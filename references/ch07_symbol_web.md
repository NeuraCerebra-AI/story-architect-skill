# Chapter 7: Symbol Web -- Analytical Summary

## How Symbols Work: The "Refer and Repeat" Principle

Truby defines a symbol as "highly concentrated meaning," the most focused condenser-expander of any storytelling technique. Where matter is concentrated energy, a symbol is concentrated feeling. The operational principle is deceptively simple: **refer and repeat**. The writer begins with a target feeling, creates a symbol that will produce that feeling in the audience, then repeats the symbol with slight variations throughout the story. Each recurrence creates a **resonance** -- ripples in a pond that expand and reverberate in the audience's mind, often without conscious awareness. The changed symbol triggers a progressively stronger feeling each time it reappears, building cumulative emotional power beneath the surface of the narrative.

## The Symbol Web: Interconnection Over Isolation

Truby identifies the single biggest mistake in symbol-making as treating a symbol as a single, isolated object -- the same error writers make when seeing a character as a single unique individual rather than part of a web. The key principle is: **always create a web of symbols in which each symbol helps define the others**. Just as the character web reveals deeper truths through comparison and contrast of people, and plot reveals truth through a causal sequence of actions, the symbol web reveals a deeper reality by **referring objects, people, and actions to other objects, people, and actions**. When the audience makes these comparisons -- even partially or fleetingly -- they perceive the deepest nature of the things being compared. The symbol web is constructed by attaching symbols to any or all of these elements: the entire story, the structure, characters, theme, story world, actions, objects, and dialogue.

## Story Symbols: Symbols at the Premise Level

A story symbol expresses the fundamental story twists, central theme, or overall structure, unifying them under a single image. Truby provides an extensive catalogue of examples. In *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, the central symbol is not the journey but the **raft** -- that fragile, floating island where a white boy and a black slave can live as friends and equals. In *Heart of Darkness*, the title's "heart of darkness" represents the physical, psychological, and moral endpoint of Marlow's trip. The titles of *Spider-Man*, *Batman*, and *Superman* describe hybrid men with special powers but also imply characters divided within themselves and separated from humanity. In *The Cherry Orchard*, the orchard suggests timeless beauty that is also impractical and expendable. In *The Scarlet Letter*, the letter begins as a mark of immorality but becomes the emblem of a different, truer morality based on real love. Each of these story symbols functions as a compressed thesis statement for the entire narrative.

## The Symbol Line: Deriving Symbols from the Designing Principle

To build a coherent symbol web, the writer must first establish a **symbol line** -- a single connecting thread linking all major symbols. This line must emerge from the designing principle, the theme line, and the story world already created. Truby demonstrates this through extended examples. In the Book of Exodus, the designing principle (a man struggling to lead his people to freedom and receiving new moral laws) yields a symbol line of "God's word made physical" -- the burning bush, the plagues, the tablets. In *A Christmas Carol*, the designing principle of forced confrontation with past, present, and future yields the symbol line of ghosts resulting in rebirth at Christmas. In *Citizen Kane*, one man's life is "made physical" through the paperweight, Xanadu, the news documentary, and the sled. The symbol line ensures that every individual symbol in the web reinforces the same fundamental story logic rather than scattering in unrelated directions.

## Symbolic Characters: Characters as Symbols

Character and symbol are two subsystems of the story body that are not separate but deeply intertwined. To connect a symbol to a character, the writer chooses a symbol that represents a **defining principle** of that character (or its reverse). The process follows six steps: (1) examine the entire character web before creating a symbol for any single character; (2) begin with the opposition between hero and main opponent; (3) identify a single aspect or emotion you want the character to evoke; (4) consider applying a **symbol opposition within the character** for complexity; (5) repeat the symbol in association with the character throughout the story; and (6) vary the detail with each repetition.

Truby identifies three broad categories of symbolic character: **gods, animals, and machines**. God symbolism connects a character to divine qualities -- Joyce's Stephen Dedalus linked to the inventor Daedalus, with the opposing symbols of Icarus (ambition leading to destruction) and the labyrinth (entrapment). Puzo's Godfather is God the Father who controls his world and metes out justice, but with the symbolic opposition of the devil. In *The Philadelphia Story*, Tracy Lord is a "bronze goddess" whose story turns on whether she will succumb to cold superiority or find her most human self.

Animal symbolism grounds characters in biologically driven behavior. In *A Streetcar Named Desire*, Stanley is called a pig, bull, ape, hound, and wolf; Blanche is connected to a moth and a bird. Eventually, "the wolf eats the bird." Comic book heroes like Batman and Spider-Man are literally animal-men -- fundamentally divided, half man and half beast.

Machine symbolism creates characters with superhuman strength but without feeling. The signature move with machine characters is **reversal**: by the story's end, the machine man proves himself the most human, while the human character has acted like an animal or a machine. Frankenstein pioneered this approach, with its triangulated hierarchy of god-man (Dr. Frankenstein), machine-man (the monster), and sub-human (the hunchback).

## Symbolic Names: The Technique of Meaningful Names

The symbolic name translates a character's essential principle into their name. Dickens was the master: Ebenezer Scrooge immediately suggests miserliness; Uriah Heep seeps with slimy falseness; Tiny Tim signals the ultimate good boy. Fitzgerald uses symbolic names in *The Great Gatsby* to satirize failed attempts at American aristocracy -- names like "Stonewall Jackson Abrams" suggest pretension, while "the Leeches" and "the Fishguards" reveal harsher truths. Truby cautions that this technique works best in **comedy**, since comedy tends toward character type, and in post-nineteenth-century fiction the method can feel too obviously thematic if handled clumsily. A related technique mixes real historical figures into fiction (as in *Ragtime* or *The Plot Against America*), exploiting the prefabricated, iconic power of famous names.

## Symbol Connected to Character Change

This advanced technique uses a symbol to **track** the character's transformation. The writer chooses a symbol representing what the character will become after undergoing change, attaches it to the character's weakness or need in the framing scene at the beginning, then brings it back -- with variation -- at the moment of character change near the end.

The supreme example is *The Godfather*. In the opening scene, the negotiation between a supplicant and Vito Corleone subtly suggests a Faustian bargain, with the final line hinting that the Godfather is the devil. Near the end, Michael stands at a christening where the priest asks, "Do you renounce Satan?" -- and Michael responds "I do renounce him" even as his men are gunning down the heads of the five families. The devil symbol tracks Michael's transformation, and because it was applied with such subtlety in the opening scene -- no one uses the word "devil" -- the technique achieves devastating dramatic impact precisely through restraint, not in spite of it.

## Symbolic Themes: Theme Expressed Through Symbols

Symbolic theme produces the most intense concentration of meaning of all symbol techniques, and is therefore the riskiest -- if done clumsily, the story feels preachy. The method requires creating an image or object that expresses a **series of actions that hurt others**. Even more powerful is an image expressing **two moral sequences in conflict**. In *The Scarlet Letter*, the letter A initially represents the moral argument against adultery but gradually comes to embody two opposing moralities: the absolute, inflexible, hypocritical public chastisement versus the fluid, true morality Hester and her lover have lived in private. In *The Great Gatsby*, Fitzgerald weaves three symbols into a thematic sequence: the green light (the perverted American dream of material wealth), the spectacles billboard before the dump (America consumed by its own mechanical refuse), and the "fresh, green breast of the new world" (natural America full of potential). Critically, this last symbol appears on the final page, out of chronological order but in perfect **structural** order -- its lush promise made shocking by contrast with what has been done to that promise.

## Symbol for Story World: The Physical World as Symbol

Natural worlds -- islands, mountains, forests, oceans -- carry inherent symbolic power, but writers can heighten or alter their meaning through additional symbolic attachment. One powerful method is to **infuse places with magical powers**, as in Prospero's island in *The Tempest*, the Forest of Arden in *As You Like It*, or the Dark Forest in Harry Potter. In *Moonstruck*, the moon becomes a physical manifestation of fate, connecting characters to forces far greater than themselves. In *Cinema Paradiso*, the movie house symbolizes both the entire story and the world: a cocoon where community is created through shared magic, which decays as the town evolves into a city. In *The Matrix* and *Network*, the title symbols ("matrix," "network") suggest a single unit that is also a web of enslaving threads, telling the audience they are entering a world of hidden forces. When a story tracks the evolution from one stage of society to another, a single well-chosen symbol can make complex social forces real, cohesive, and understandable -- as with the buffalo in *She Wore a Yellow Ribbon*, symbolizing the vanishing frontier.

## Symbolic Actions: Actions That Carry Symbolic Meaning

Making an action symbolic connects it to another action or object, charging it with heightened meaning and making it stand out from the plot sequence. It says, in effect, "This action is especially important and expresses the theme or character of the story in miniature." In *Wuthering Heights*, Heathcliff's pretend fight against the black knight for Cathy plays out, in miniature, the entire story's struggle. In *Witness*, John Book helping to build a barn while exchanging glances with Rachel signals his willingness to leave violence for a loving community of peace. In *A Tale of Two Cities*, Sydney Carton's willing sacrifice at the guillotine is a Christ-like symbolic action. Because symbolic actions call attention to themselves, Truby advises careful, sparing use.

## Symbolic Objects: Objects as Symbols

Symbolic objects almost never function alone because a single object has almost no ability to refer to something else. The writer must create a **web of objects** related by a guiding principle, with each object referring not only outward but also to every other symbolic object in the story. The construction begins by returning to the designing principle, which serves as the glue transforming a collection of individual objects into a cluster. Truby examines three highly metaphorical genre forms. In **myth**, objects like the journey (life path), labyrinth (confusion on the path to enlightenment), garden (harmony), tree (tree of life), talisman (right action), and underground (unexplored self) carry culturally inherited meanings. In the **horror** genre, the symbol web starts from the binary of light versus dark -- the cross on one side, dark animals (wolf, bat, snake) on the other. In the **Western**, the symbol web includes the horseman (the warrior ideal), the six-gun (mechanized justice), the hat (white for good, black for evil), the badge/star (law enforcement), and the fence (fragile civilization). Truby's analysis of *Shane* demonstrates how these objects form a clean, interconnected system.

## Reversing the Symbol: The Advanced Technique of Symbol Reversal

The great flaw of a prefabricated, highly metaphorical symbol web is that it becomes predictable -- a blueprint rather than a lived experience. But this flaw contains a tremendous opportunity. The writer can exploit the audience's knowledge of the form by **using all the standard symbols but twisting their meaning** so it diverges sharply from expectation. This forces the audience to rethink all their assumptions. In genre storytelling, this is known as **undercutting the genre**.

Truby's primary case study is *McCabe and Mrs. Miller*, which systematically reverses every classic Western symbol. The hero is not a lawman but a gambler who builds a town through "the capitalism of sex." His love interest is not the pure frontier wife but an opium-smoking madam. The town is not a rational grid on a dry southwestern plain but a makeshift settlement in the rainy Northwest forest. The showdown does not happen on the main street in full view of the community; instead, McCabe is chased through a blinding snowstorm while the townspeople are off fighting a church fire. The hero's weapon is not the noble six-gun but a hidden derringer -- "in classic Westerns, the weapon of women." His costume is not chaps and a white hat but an eastern suit and a bowler. By reversing every expected symbol, the film transforms old metaphorical conventions into something startlingly original, making *McCabe and Mrs. Miller* both an education in great storytelling and a landmark of American cinema.
