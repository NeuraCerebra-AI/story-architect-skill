# Chapter 3: The Seven Key Steps of Story Structure

## Analytical Summary

The seven key structure steps are not imposed from outside like a mechanical three-act framework. They are organic -- embedded in the story's DNA, derived from the steps any human being must work through to solve a life problem. Because they are organic, they must be linked properly to achieve maximum impact.

---

## 1. The Seven Steps in Detail

### Step 1: Weakness and Need

The hero begins the story with one or more great weaknesses so profound they are ruining his life. The **need** is what the hero must fulfill within himself to have a better life -- it usually involves overcoming these weaknesses and growing.

**Critical rules:**
- The hero must **not** be aware of his need at the beginning. If he already knows what he needs, the story is over. Awareness of need arrives only at the self-revelation, near the end, after great pain (drama) or struggle (comedy).
- The hero should have both a **psychological need** and a **moral need** (see Section 3 below).

Also present from page one is the **problem** -- an external crisis the hero is very aware of but cannot solve. The problem should be an outward manifestation of the hero's internal weakness. It gives the story a fast start and defines the character quickly. Keep it simple and specific.

**Example -- Tootsie:** Weaknesses: arrogant, selfish, a liar. Need: overcome arrogance toward women and stop lying to get what he wants. Problem: he is so overbearing no one will hire him.

### Step 2: Desire

Desire is the hero's specific goal in this story -- not what he wants in life generally, but what drives his actions in this particular narrative. Desire is the surface track the audience rides; it is what they think the story is about. Need remains hidden underneath.

**Critical rules:**
- Desire must be **specific and singular** -- a concrete goal requiring a series of actions.
- Desire must extend through the entire story, pulling the hero (and the audience) forward.
- Never confuse need and desire or collapse them into one step. Need is internal (overcoming a flaw); desire is external (pursuing a goal).
- Never skip weakness-and-need to open with desire. Starting with desire gives a quick opening but kills the payoff -- without need, the hero cannot change, and the story loses its personal meaning.

**Example -- The Verdict:** Need: regain self-respect (psychological) and learn to act with justice (moral). Desire: win the case.

### Step 3: Opponent

The opponent is not simply the character who looks or acts evil. Defined structurally, the true opponent wants to **prevent the hero from achieving his desire** and is **competing for the same goal**. This forces repeated direct conflict throughout the story.

**Critical rules:**
- Start with the hero's specific goal; whoever wants to keep him from it is the opponent.
- Find the **deepest level of conflict** between hero and opponent -- the most important thing they are fighting about.
- Never define the opponent as "the hero fighting himself." Internal struggle is a weakness, not an opponent. Externalizing opposition into a separate character is structurally essential.
- The opponent can be moral, sympathetic, even the hero's lover or friend.

**Example -- Chinatown:** Jake and Noah Cross compete over whose version of the truth will be believed. Cross wants everyone to think Hollis drowned accidentally; Jake wants to expose Cross as murderer and rapist.

### Step 4: Plan

The plan is the set of strategies the hero will use to overcome the opponent and reach the goal. It is organically linked to both desire and opponent -- it must be specifically focused on defeating the opponent and achieving the goal.

**Critical rules:**
- The plan must involve many steps (otherwise the story is too short).
- The plan must be complex enough that the hero will need to adjust when it fails.
- In some genres (caper, war story), the plan is elaborate and visually shown; in others, it may be vague.

**Example -- Hamlet:** Stage a play mimicking the murder of his father; prove the king's guilt by his reaction.

### Step 5: Battle

Throughout the middle of the story, hero and opponent engage in escalating punch-counterpunch conflict. The **battle** is the final confrontation that determines who wins the goal. It may be a conflict of violence or a conflict of words.

**Critical rules:**
- The battle must involve the hero and the main opponent directly.
- It must decide once and for all who wins the goal.
- It must be an intense experience that puts the hero to the ultimate test.

**Example -- The Verdict:** Frank defeats opposing counsel through brilliant lawyering and persuasive courtroom argument.

### Step 6: Self-Revelation

The crucible of battle forces the hero to have a major revelation about who he really is. In a **psychological self-revelation**, the hero strips away his facade and sees himself honestly for the first time -- the most active, difficult, and courageous act in the entire story. In a **moral self-revelation**, the hero also gains insight into the proper way to act toward others, realizes he has been wrong and has hurt people, and proves he has changed through new moral action.

**Critical rules:**
- Do not have the hero state outright what he learned -- it becomes preachy. Suggest the insight through actions leading to the revelation. (Exception: if the statement is clever or funny enough, as in Tootsie, it can work.)
- Structurally, self-revelation is most closely connected to need. Need is the beginning of character change; self-revelation is the endpoint.

**Example -- Adventures of Huckleberry Finn:** Huck realizes he has been wrong to think of Jim as less than human and declares he would rather go to hell than betray Jim's whereabouts.

### Step 7: New Equilibrium

Everything returns to normal and all desire is gone, except for one major difference: the hero has moved to a **higher or lower level** as a result of the crucible. A positive self-revelation lifts the hero; a negative revelation (or the inability to have one) causes the hero to fall or be destroyed.

**Example of rising -- The Silence of the Lambs:** Clarice has brought Buffalo Bill to justice, become an excellent FBI agent, and apparently conquered her nightmares.

**Example of falling -- Vertigo:** The hero drags the woman he loves to a tower to force a confession, then watches in horror as she falls to her death.

## 2. How the Steps Are Linked

The seven steps form an unbreakable organic chain. Each step grows from and depends on the one before it:

- **Weakness/Need** establishes what is missing; it sets up every subsequent step.
- **Desire** grows from need -- when the hero achieves the goal, he typically fulfills his need. But desire is external and visible; need is internal and hidden.
- **Opponent** is defined by desire -- whoever competes for the same goal forces repeated conflict.
- **Plan** is shaped by both desire and opponent -- it is the strategy for defeating the opponent and reaching the goal.
- **Battle** is the culmination of the plan-versus-opponent conflict.
- **Self-revelation** is triggered by the crucible of battle and resolves need.
- **New equilibrium** reflects whether the self-revelation was positive or negative.

The strongest structural link is between **need** (beginning) and **self-revelation** (end). Together they bracket the hero's entire character arc.

---

## 3. Psychological vs. Moral Need

A **psychological need** involves overcoming a serious internal flaw that hurts nobody but the hero. A **moral need** involves overcoming a flaw that causes the hero to hurt other people. The moral weakness must be visible at the beginning of the story -- the audience must see the hero actively harming others.

Giving the hero a moral need is essential for two reasons: (1) it increases the scope of the character so his actions affect others, which moves the audience more powerfully; and (2) it prevents the hero from being perfect or a victim, both fatal to storytelling.

**Example -- The Verdict:** Psychological need: beat his drinking and regain self-respect. Moral need: stop using others for money and learn to act with justice. Evidence of moral weakness: he lies his way into a stranger's funeral to solicit business.

---

## 4. Types of Self-Revelation

- **Positive revelation:** The hero sees who he truly is, learns how to live properly, and rises to a higher level. (Casablanca: Rick sheds cynicism, regains idealism, sacrifices personal love for a greater cause.)
- **Negative revelation:** The hero learns something so painful it destroys him, or he proves incapable of revelation at all, and falls. (Chinatown: Jake mumbles "As little as possible," believing his life is useless and destructive.)
- **Both positive and negative:** (Dances with Wolves: Dunbar finds a new reason to live through his Lakota family, but their way of life is nearly at an end.)
- **No self-revelation given to the hero, displaced to another character:** An advanced technique. (The Godfather: Michael has no psychological or moral revelation; the moral revelation is given to Kay, who sees what he has become as the door closes in her face.)

---

## 5. Key Rules Summary

| Step | Rule |
|------|------|
| Weakness/Need | Hero must NOT be aware of his need at the start. Give both psychological and moral need. |
| Desire | Must be specific, singular, and extend through the entire story. Never skip need to start with desire. |
| Opponent | Must compete for the same goal as the hero. Find the deepest conflict. Never make the opponent "the hero himself." |
| Plan | Must involve many steps and be complex enough to require adjustment when it fails. |
| Battle | Must involve hero and main opponent directly; must decide once and for all who wins the goal. |
| Self-Revelation | Do not have the hero explicitly state the lesson. Suggest through action. Give both psychological and moral revelation. |
| New Equilibrium | Show whether the hero has risen or fallen as a permanent result of the journey. |

## 6. Worked Examples

**The Godfather (full seven-step breakdown):**
- Hero: Michael Corleone. Weaknesses: young, inexperienced, overconfident. Psychological need: overcome superiority and self-righteousness. Moral need: avoid becoming ruthless while protecting his family. Problem: rival gang members shoot his father. Desire: take revenge and protect the family. Opponent: Barzini (hidden power behind Sollozzo). Plan: kill Sollozzo and the police captain; later, kill all rival family heads in a single strike. Battle: crosscut baptism and massacre -- Michael renounces Satan while his men execute every enemy. Self-revelation: none -- displaced to Kay. New equilibrium: Michael has "risen" to Godfather but morally fallen, becoming the very evil he once rejected.

**Additional examples cited:** Tootsie, Chinatown, The Silence of the Lambs, The Verdict, Saving Private Ryan, The Full Monty, Star Wars, Sunset Boulevard, Hamlet, The Odyssey, Big, Casablanca, Dances with Wolves, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, Die Hard, Pretty Woman, Oedipus the King, The Conversation, Vertigo.

## 7. Techniques: The Seven-Steps Method

### Creating Moral Need
1. Begin with the psychological weakness.
2. Determine what immoral action naturally flows from it.
3. Identify the deep-seated moral weakness and need behind that action.

Alternative technique -- push a strength until it becomes a weakness:
1. Identify a virtue; make the character so passionate about it that it becomes oppressive.
2. Find the negative version of a value the character believes in.

### The Writing Exercise (How to Use the Seven Steps)
1. Write down 5-15 story events in single sentences.
2. Order them roughly from beginning to end.
3. **Start at the end:** determine the self-revelation first, then go back to determine need and desire. This guarantees the hero always heads toward the true structural endpoint.
4. Give the hero both psychological and moral self-revelation; be specific about what he learns.
5. Go back to the beginning: give both psychological and moral weakness and need. Create many serious weaknesses.
6. Define the problem as an outgrowth of the weakness.
7. Make desire specific and capable of sustaining the full story.
8. Create an opponent who wants the same goal and is exceptionally skilled at attacking the hero's greatest weakness.
9. Build a multi-step plan that requires adjustment when it fails.
10. Design a battle that puts the hero to the ultimate test against the main opponent.

### Key Structural Insight
Figuring out the seven steps is like doing a crossword puzzle: use the parts that come easily to unlock the difficult parts, and be willing to revise earlier decisions when later material reveals a better direction.
