# Chapter 5: Moral Argument -- Theme

## Analytical Summary of John Truby's *The Anatomy of Story*

---

## 1. Theme vs. Subject Matter

Truby draws a sharp line between theme and subject matter. Most writers conflate the two, treating theme as a topical label -- death, redemption, love, class, corruption. Truby rejects this entirely. Theme is the author's personal view of how to act in the world. It is a moral vision, not a category. Whenever a character uses means to reach an end, the author is presenting a moral predicament and making an argument about the right way to live.

Using the body metaphor that runs through the book, Truby positions theme as the brain of the story body. Character is the heart, structure is the skeleton, and theme is the higher design intelligence. It must lead the writing process without becoming so dominant that the story collapses into a philosophical thesis. The spectrum ranges from heavily thematic forms (drama, allegory, irony, "serious literature") to popular forms (adventure, myth, fantasy, action) where theme is slight and emphasis falls on surprise, suspense, and emotion rather than moral complexity.

The critical warning: average writers express moral vision almost exclusively through dialogue, producing preachy, "on the nose" stories. Good writers express it primarily through story structure -- through how the hero pursues the goal, competes with opponents, and what the hero learns or fails to learn.

---

## 2. The Theme Line

The theme line condenses the author's moral vision into a single sentence. It states the writer's view about right and wrong actions and what those actions do to a person's life. Though it can seem heavy-handed as a standalone sentence, the theme line forces the writer to focus all moral elements into one governing idea.

The theme line derives from the designing principle. The technique is to examine the designing principle's key actions strictly for their moral effects: How do the characters' actions hurt other people? How, if at all, do they make things right?

Truby identifies several designing-principle techniques that yield strong theme lines:

- **Traveling/Journey**: Embeds an entire moral sequence into the line. Huck's trip down the Mississippi is simultaneously a trip into deeper slavery. The journey in *King Kong* from Manhattan to Skull Island and back reveals that both places are governed by cutthroat competition, with human civilization being more brutal.
- **Single Grand Symbol**: The scarlet letter "A" stands for adultery on the surface, but more deeply for the townspeople's hidden sins and their attack on true love through public conformity. The tolling bell in Hemingway implies that sacrificing for loved ones is what gives life meaning in the face of death.
- **Connecting Two Grand Symbols**: Two symbols mark the poles of a moral sequence, usually declining. *Heart of Darkness* uses both twin symbols and the journey metaphor together.

Worked examples of designing principle to theme line include *It's a Wonderful Life* ("A man's riches come not from money but from friends and family he serves"), *Citizen Kane* ("A man who tries to force everyone to love him ends up alone"), *The Sting* ("A little lying and cheating are OK if you bring down an evil man"), and *A Christmas Carol* ("A person lives a much happier life when he gives to others").

---

## 3. Moral Argument Through Structure

One of the great principles of storytelling, Truby asserts, is that structure does not just carry content -- it is content. And structural content is far more powerful than anything characters say. The mechanism works through convergence and expansion: as story structure converges toward the climax, theme expands in the audience's mind.

At the beginning, hero and opponent are set in opposition but the conflict is mild and the audience has almost no sense of theme. Through the middle, conflict intensifies and differing values emerge; theme grows quietly. At the convergent point -- the battle, self-revelation, and moral decision -- theme explodes. Because it has been expressed primarily through structure, it seems to emerge from the audience's own soul rather than being imposed like a sermon.

**The Basic Strategy of Moral Argument through structure:**

1. **Values**: The hero starts with a set of beliefs and values.
2. **Moral Weakness**: The hero hurts others, acting from weakness or ignorance.
3. **Moral Need**: The hero must learn how to act properly toward others.
4. **First Immoral Action**: Early evidence of the hero's moral flaw.
5. **Desire**: A goal that leads the hero into conflict with an opponent holding different values.
6. **Drive and Immoral Actions**: Losing to the opponent, the hero becomes desperate and takes increasingly immoral steps. Allies criticize; the hero justifies.
7. **Attack by Ally**: The hero's closest friend makes the strongest case against the hero's methods.
8. **Obsessive Drive**: The hero becomes consumed with winning and will do almost anything.
9. **Battle**: The final conflict decides which set of values is superior.
10. **Moral Self-Revelation**: The hero recognizes how he has been wrong toward others.
11. **Moral Decision**: The hero chooses between two courses of action, proving the self-revelation.
12. **Thematic Revelation**: An insight for the audience about how people in general should act -- breaking the bounds of these particular characters and affecting the audience where they live. This is the highest expression of theme.

Truby demonstrates this full sequence through *The Verdict*, where Frank Galvin's moral self-revelation and decision to reject a settlement for justice illustrate the technique -- though Truby notes that placing the self-revelation only 25 minutes in weakens the moral argument by removing moral jeopardy too early. The *Iliad* doubles the sequence: Achilles declines and rises once with Agamemnon, then repeats the pattern more intensely with Hector, achieving a deeper self-revelation about compassion over vengeance.

---

## 4. Splitting the Theme into Oppositions

Truby identifies three techniques for breaking the theme line into dramatic oppositions:

- **The Hero's Moral Decision**: The final choice between two actions is the narrow end of the funnel for theme. Examples include Rick choosing the fight against Nazis over Ilsa (*Casablanca*), Spade choosing justice over the woman he loves (*The Maltese Falcon*), and Scottie's failure to forgive in *Vertigo*.
- **Characters as Variations on a Theme**: Every major character deals with the same central moral problem in a different way. In *Tootsie*, every character represents a variation on how men treat women. In *L.A. Confidential*, three detectives embody different corruptions of justice.
- **Characters' Values in Conflict**: Each major character holds a distinct cluster of values that come into direct conflict as they compete for the same goal. In *It's a Wonderful Life*, George Bailey represents democracy, decency, and the common man; Potter represents one-man rule, money, and survival of the fittest. In *The Cherry Orchard*, Ranevsky values beauty and the past; Lopakhin values money and the future.

Truby strongly advocates **four-corner opposition** -- a hero, a main opponent, and at least two secondary opponents -- to avoid simplistic two-part moral frameworks. A web of moral oppositions gives the audience a sense of the moral complexity of real life.

---

## 5. Variants of Moral Argument

### (1) Good vs. Bad
The simplest form. The hero remains essentially good; the opponent remains bad. The hero wins because he is good. Common in myth, action, and melodrama. Examples: *Star Wars*, *The Matrix*, *Field of Dreams*, *Shane*, *The Wizard of Oz*, *Forrest Gump*.

### (2) Tragedy
The hero has great potential but also a great (fatal) flaw. The hero declines morally through obsessive competition, then gains a self-revelation that comes too late to avoid destruction. The key elements are the sense of might-have-been (which generates sympathy) and the hero's responsibility for his own fall (which prevents him from becoming a victim). The audience feels sadness at lost potential but also inspiration from the hero's moral and emotional success achieved moments too late.

Truby's extended analysis of *Wuthering Heights* shows Bronte reversing heroes mid-story, giving Heathcliff the lead after Cathy's tragic choice. Heathcliff's return armed with wealth generates audience triumph, but Bronte then reverses the moral argument by having Heathcliff go too far -- marrying innocents for revenge. In *King Lear*, Shakespeare doubles the tragic fall (Lear and Gloucester both decline and die) to suggest moral blindness endemic to the human race, then kills the one morally good character (Cordelia) to leave only a sliver of hope.

### (3) Pathos
The tragic hero is reduced to an everyman. The hero has atrophied, out-of-date values and a goal beyond his grasp. The opponent is an impersonal system too powerful to comprehend. The hero gets no self-revelation -- he is not capable of one -- but keeps fighting. He ends broken, dead of heartbreak, or by suicide. The audience feels deep injustice and admiration for the beautiful failure. Examples: *Death of a Salesman*, *Don Quixote*, *A Streetcar Named Desire*, *The Conversation*, *Ikiru*.

### (4) Satire and Irony
Satire is the comedy of societal beliefs; irony is the grand pattern where characters get the opposite of what they pursue. The technique sets up a constant contrast between characters who think they are moral (upholding society's values) and the decidedly immoral effects of their actions. The hero believes in the system, pursues ambition or romance, and the competition produces silly and destructive results. The self-revelation is often undercut, and the hero's moral action has little effect on the larger system's foolishness.

Truby's detailed analysis of Austen's *Emma* demonstrates the full satiric-ironic sequence: Emma's matchmaking, driven by class assumptions and self-deception, produces cascading harm (Harriet loses a good man, Mr. Elton is crushed, Miss Bates is humiliated). Mr. Knightley provides moral correction at key moments. The marriage at the end subtly undercuts the class system even while reaffirming it.

### (5) Black Comedy
The most advanced and difficult form. It shows destruction resulting not from individual choice (tragedy) but from individuals trapped in an innately destructive system. The critical technique: the self-revelation is withheld from the hero and given instead to the audience. Many characters pursue a negative, often murderous goal with insane but detailed justifications. One sane person (the ally/chorus) points out the madness; no one listens. The battle is intense and destructive; no one learns anything; survivors resume their efforts. The writer must make the hero likable to prevent the audience from feeling morally superior. Examples: *Dr. Strangelove*, *Network*, *Catch-22*, *Goodfellas*, *Brazil*.

---

## 6. Balancing Moral Argument with Plot

The single biggest reason a story feels preachy is an imbalance between moral argument and plot. Even perfectly sequenced moral argument expressed through structure will collapse without sufficient plot to support it. Plot -- the intricate choreography of actions designed to surprise the audience -- is the element of surprise and magic that floats the moral sequence. Without enough plot, the moral argument becomes a sermonizing bore.

---

## 7. Moral Argument in Dialogue

While structure does the heavy lifting, dialogue provides subtlety and emotional force. Truby identifies three key places for moral dialogue:

1. **Ally criticizes the hero**: The hero's closest friend challenges the hero's immoral methods. The hero, not yet having had a self-revelation, defends his actions.
2. **Conflict between hero and opponent**: Most likely during the battle scene, though early moral arguments between hero and opponent (as in *It's a Wonderful Life*, where George confronts Potter about the Building and Loan) give the audience early clues about what values are at stake.
3. **The opponent's moral justification**: The most advanced technique. The opponent gives a strong, compelling (though flawed) argument for his actions. This avoids the straw-man trap and gives depth to both opponent and hero. Concannon in *The Verdict* ("We're paid to win"), Colonel Jessup in *A Few Good Men*, Uncle Charlie in *Shadow of a Doubt* -- each offers chilling but internally coherent justifications. The rule: never give the opponent an obviously weak argument; give him the best argument possible, but ensure it has a fatal flaw in its logic.

---

## 8. The Unique Moral Vision

At the most advanced level, a writer creates a moral system that is original and comprehensive. Hawthorne in *The Scarlet Letter* establishes a three-person opposition making the case for natural morality based on real love. Joyce in *Ulysses* creates a natural religion and everyday heroism through a day trip across Dublin.

This level also appears in blockbuster films, though in simpler form. *Star Wars* blends Eastern and Western morality into the Force -- a creed audiences could live by. *The Godfather* lays out a moral system based on modern business and warfare, producing taglines ("It's not personal, it's business") that function as a modern catechism. Truby emphasizes that the attempt to build a moral system, even in shorthand, is a major source of these stories' enduring appeal and should not be dismissed as mere spectacle.

---

## 9. Combining Moral Arguments

Advanced storytellers combine variant forms in a single story, though this is extremely difficult because the different forms work in different ways and produce different emotional effects. *Ulysses* starts with good-versus-bad myth and deepens it with satiric-ironic argument. *The Cherry Orchard* combines pathos with satire-irony. *American Beauty* attempts tragedy, black comedy, and satire together but never fully realizes any single form's potential -- illustrating the risk of combination. Other mixed examples include *Madame Bovary*, *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, and *Dog Day Afternoon*.

---

## 10. Worked Example: Casablanca (Full Moral Argument)

Truby traces the complete moral argument sequence through *Casablanca*:

- **Theme line**: Even a great love may have to be sacrificed to fight oppression.
- **Moral problem**: Balancing personal desire against sacrifice for the larger good.
- **Characters as variations**: Rick (selfish isolationist), Ilsa (torn between love and duty), Laszlo (will sacrifice anything for the cause), Renault (pure opportunist).
- **Moral decline**: Rick accepts stolen transit letters, refuses to help Ugarte, verbally abuses Ilsa, propositions her, rejects Laszlo's offers out of spite.
- **Criticism and justification**: Rick's justifications ("I stick my neck out for nobody") track his cynicism; allies and opponents provide escalating moral pressure.
- **Self-revelation**: Rick realizes his love for Ilsa is not as important as helping Laszlo fight Nazi domination.
- **Moral decision**: Rick gives Laszlo the letters, forces Ilsa to leave with her husband, and joins the Free French.
- **Thematic revelation**: Renault's surprise flip -- deciding to join Rick in the fight -- delivers the universal insight: in the battle against fascism, everyone must play a part.

This double reversal at the end is the thematic revelation breaking beyond individual characters and touching the audience with a vision of collective moral responsibility.
