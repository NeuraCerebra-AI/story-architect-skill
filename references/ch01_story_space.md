# Chapter 1: Story Space -- Analytical Summary

## 1. Core Concepts

- **Story is organic, not mechanical.** A great story is a living body that develops, not a machine assembled from parts. Every element connects to and feeds off every other element.
- **Story as verbal game.** The storyteller plays a verbal game with the audience -- making up characters and actions, selectively revealing and withholding information so the audience is drawn in to figure out the puzzle.
- **Story delivers emotional knowledge.** Good storytelling does not merely report events; it gives the audience the *experience* of a life, conveying wisdom in a playful, entertaining way.
- **Desire drives story.** The story world operates on "I want, therefore I am." Desire propels all conscious beings and gives them direction. A story tracks what a person wants, what he will do to get it, and the costs he pays.
- **Story walks on two legs: acting and learning.** A character pursuing a desire takes actions and learns new information, which causes him to change course. All stories move through this dual mechanism.
- **The story world is not the real world.** Stories show life as human beings *imagine it could be* -- condensed and heightened so the audience gains better understanding of how life works.
- **Every element is defined relationally.** No individual element (including the hero) works unless it is created and defined in relation to all other elements in the story.

---

## 2. Techniques

- **Reveal and withhold.** The storyteller creates a puzzle by telling the audience certain information about a character and hiding other information. When the audience no longer needs to figure anything out, the story stops.
- **Selecting, connecting, and building intense moments.** Storytelling is not remembering past events; it is curating a series of charged moments the listener feels as lived experience.
- **Building from the inside out.** Construct the story by (1) making it personal and unique to the author and (2) finding and developing what is original within the story idea. Each layer grows from the prior one.
- **Organic writing process (internal, interconnected, original).** Contrasted with the common flawed process (external, mechanical, piecemeal, generic). Details below under Writing Process Steps.
- **Character defined through comparison.** Connect and compare each character to every other character so that each one is strong and well defined.
- **Theme expressed through structure, not dialogue.** Avoid making characters mouthpieces for messages; embed the moral argument in the story's structure so it surprises and moves the audience.
- **Story world as hero's externalization.** Create the world as an outgrowth of the hero; it physically expresses his growth.
- **Symbol web.** Symbols are packets of highly compressed meaning; weave a web of them to highlight characters, story world, and plot.
- **Symphonic dialogue.** Dialogue that does not just push plot forward but blends many "instruments" and levels simultaneously, like a symphony.

---

## 3. Rules and Guidelines

- **Reject mechanical story models.** Three-act structure is external to the story and has nothing to do with its internal logic. It leads to episodic storytelling where events do not connect or build.
- **Abandon classical terminology as a creative tool.** Terms like "rising action," "climax," "progressive complication," and "denouement" are too broad and theoretical to have practical value for storytellers.
- **Never write episodically.** An episodic story is a collection of disconnected pieces stored in a box; it moves the audience sporadically, if at all.
- **Do not focus solely on the hero.** Treating the opponent and minor characters as separate from and less important than the hero produces weak, poorly defined characters.
- **Do not avoid theme.** Avoiding theme entirely or expressing it only through dialogue are both failures. Theme must be woven into structure.
- **Do not ask "What happens next?" as the sole plot driver.** That question alone produces episodic, surface-level plot without deep causal connections.
- **Make technique invisible.** Like a great athlete whose mastery makes everything look effortless, the storyteller must master craft until it disappears from view, and the audience sees only the living story.
- **Change must be the focal point.** The dramatic code targets the moment of change -- the impact where a person breaks free of habits, weaknesses, and ghosts from the past.

---

## 4. Key Terms and Definitions

- **Premise**: The entire story condensed to a single sentence; the shortest expression of the story as a whole.
- **Dramatic Code**: The deep artistic description of how a person can grow or evolve, embedded in the human psyche and running beneath every story (see Section 6 below).
- **Organic Story**: A story that functions as a living body -- multiple subsystems (characters, plot, revelations, story world, moral argument, symbol web, scene weave, symphonic dialogue) all interconnected.
- **Episodic Story**: A story whose events stand out as discrete, unconnected elements that do not build from beginning to end.
- **Seven Key Story Structure Steps**: The major stages of a story's development; the story's DNA. (Detailed in later chapters.)
- **Twenty-Two Story Structure Steps**: The seven key steps plus fifteen additional steps used to design a fully connected plot.
- **Scene Weave**: A list of every scene with all plotlines and themes woven into a tapestry.
- **Symphonic Dialogue**: Dialogue that blends multiple thematic, emotional, and narrative levels simultaneously.
- **Story World**: Not a copy of reality but a heightened, condensed version of life imagined through the lens of the hero.
- **Moral Argument (Theme)**: The author's moral vision of how people should act in the world, expressed through structure rather than dialogue.
- **Symbol Web**: An interconnected set of symbols highlighting different aspects of characters, world, and plot.
- **Designing Principle**: The deeper logic or "what if" that organizes the story idea (illustrated by the Jurassic Park example: "What if you took the two greatest heavyweights of evolution and forced them to fight to the death in the same ring?").

## 5. Story Movement Types

Truby identifies five natural patterns of movement that storytellers use (individually and in combination) to connect events over time:

- **Linear**: Tracks a single main character from beginning to end on a straight-line path. Implies a historical or biological explanation. Most Hollywood films are linear -- a single hero pursuing a desire with great intensity.
- **Meandering**: Follows a winding path without apparent direction (like rivers, snakes, the brain). The hero's desire is not intense; he covers great territory haphazardly and encounters characters from different social levels. Examples: *Odyssey*, *Don Quixote*, *Huckleberry Finn*, *David Copperfield*.
- **Spiral**: A path circling inward to the center (like cyclones, horns, seashells). A character returns to a single event or memory and explores it at progressively deeper levels. Examples: *Vertigo*, *Blow-Up*, *The Conversation*, *Memento*.
- **Branching**: Paths extend from central points by splitting into smaller parts (like trees, leaves, river basins). Each branch represents a complete society or a detailed stage of society. Found in social fantasies and multi-hero stories. Examples: *Gulliver's Travels*, *Nashville*, *American Graffiti*, *Traffic*.
- **Explosive (Simultaneous)**: Multiple paths extending simultaneously (like volcanoes, dandelions). Strictly impossible in narrative (you must tell one thing after another), but the appearance of simultaneity is achieved through crosscutting. Implies a comparative explanation. Stories emphasizing simultaneity tend to use branching structure. Examples: *Pulp Fiction*, *Syriana*, *Crash*, *Ulysses*, *The Canterbury Tales*, *L.A. Confidential*, *Hannah and Her Sisters*.

**Key insight**: Linear and explosive are the two extremes. Meandering, spiral, and branching are combinations of the two.

## 6. The Dramatic Code

The dramatic code is Truby's central concept for what lies beneath all stories:

- It is an **artistic description of how a person can grow or evolve**, embedded deep in the human psyche.
- It is a **process running underneath every story**, hidden beneath particular characters and actions.
- **Change is fueled by desire.** A character wants something, acts to get it, encounters impediments, struggles, and is forced to change (or fails to change).
- **The code walks on two legs**: acting (taking actions to pursue desire) and learning (gaining new information that alters the course of action).
- **Drama is a code of maturity.** The focal point is the moment of change -- when a person breaks free of old habits, weaknesses, and ghosts, transforming into a richer, fuller self.
- **Different story forms frame human change differently**:
  - Myth: widest arc, birth to death, animal to divine.
  - Plays: focus on the main character's moment of decision.
  - Film (especially American): small change through pursuing a limited goal with great intensity.
  - Classic short stories: a few events leading to a single important insight.
  - Serious novels: how a person interacts and changes within an entire society, or the precise mental/emotional processes of change.
  - Television drama: multiple characters in a mini-society struggling to change simultaneously.

## 7. Writing Process Steps (Organic Method)

Truby prescribes a sequential, inside-out writing process. Each step grows naturally from the previous one:

1. **Premise** -- Condense the entire story into a single sentence. Use it to discover the story's essence and determine how to develop the idea for maximum impact.
2. **Seven Key Story Structure Steps** -- Identify the major stages of development (the story's DNA) to give it a solid, stable foundation.
3. **Character** -- Draw characters out of the original story idea (not from thin air). Connect and compare each character to every other character. Define each character's function in helping the hero develop.
4. **Theme (Moral Argument)** -- Express the theme inherent in the story idea through story structure, not through dialogue or message. It must surprise and move the audience.
5. **Story World** -- Create the world as an outgrowth of the hero. Use it to define the hero and physically express his growth.
6. **Symbol Web** -- Design a web of symbols that highlight and communicate aspects of characters, story world, and plot.
7. **Plot** -- Discover the right story form from the characters. Using the twenty-two structure steps (seven key + fifteen more), design a plot where all events are connected beneath the surface and build to a surprising but logically necessary ending.
8. **Scene Weave** -- Create a list of every scene in the story with all plotlines and themes woven together.
9. **Scene Construction and Symphonic Dialogue** -- Write each scene to further the hero's development. Craft dialogue that blends multiple instruments and levels simultaneously.

**Contrasting flawed process** (what most writers do): Start with a generic premise copied from existing work. Flesh out the hero mechanically with tacked-on traits. Ignore or neglect the opponent and minor characters. Avoid theme or state it bluntly in dialogue. Default to a familiar story world. Skip symbols. Plot by asking only "What happens next?" Organize with three-act structure. Result: episodic, generic, formulaic work without deep audience impact.
