# Chapter 6: Story World -- Analytical Summary

## 1. Story World as Character Expression

Truby's central thesis on story world inverts real life: whereas human beings are born into a pre-existing world and must adapt to it, in great storytelling the characters come first and the writer designs the world as "an infinitely detailed manifestation of those characters." T.S. Eliot called this the "objective correlative"--the physical world becomes a direct expression of the character web and especially of the hero. The story world functions as a "condenser-expander": it compresses vast amounts of meaning (characters, plot, symbols, moral argument) into limited space and time, which then expands in the audience's mind as story elements ricochet against one another. Gaston Bachelard's insight is foundational here--intimate space and exterior space "keep encouraging each other in their growth." When you create the right world, you plant seeds in the audience's hearts that grow organically. The process requires speaking two languages simultaneously--words and images--and matching them exactly across the entire story.

## 2. The Arena

The arena is the basic space of drama: a single, unified place surrounded by some kind of wall. Everything inside is part of the story; everything outside is not. Truby warns that breaking the single arena dissipates drama--too many arenas produce fragmented, inorganic stories. There are four major strategies for maintaining a unified arena:

1. **Large Umbrella, Then Crosscut and Condense**: Establish the broadest scope early (a city, a plain, outer space, a house), then focus on smaller subworlds within it. Used in *Casablanca*, *Star Wars*, *The Great Gatsby*, *It's a Wonderful Life*.

2. **Single-Line Journey**: The hero travels through fundamentally similar terrain that develops along a recognizable line, giving the sense of unity. Used in *Titanic*, *The Wild Bunch*, *The African Queen*.

3. **Circular Journey**: The hero departs from home and returns, highlighting character change against a world that has remained the same. Used in *The Wizard of Oz*, *Ulysses*, *Finding Nemo*, *King Kong*, *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*.

4. **Fish Out of Water**: The hero begins in one arena, demonstrates unique talents, then jumps (without traveling) to a second, superficially different arena where those same talents apply. Used in *Beverly Hills Cop*, *Crocodile Dundee*, *Witness*. A key rule: do not linger too long in the first arena.

## 3. Visual Oppositions

You do not create characters to fill a story world; you create a story world to express and manifest your characters. Visual oppositions in the physical world are derived directly from the oppositions among characters and their values. Return to the character web, identify how characters fight and what values they clash over, and tease out the three or four central visual oppositions.

Examples demonstrate the technique vividly. In *It's a Wonderful Life*, the two versions of the same town--Bedford Falls versus Pottersville--are direct physical manifestations of the opposing values of George Bailey (democracy, decency, kindness) and Mr. Potter (greed, one-man rule). In *The Great Gatsby*, Fitzgerald creates layered visual oppositions: flat Midwest plains versus tall Eastern towers; East Egg (older money) versus West Egg (nouveau riche); Tom's conservative mansion versus Gatsby's garish palace; the gas station of Tom's mistress versus his mansion; the city of ashes versus gleaming New York. In *Dances with Wolves*, the visual oppositions shift as the story progresses--Dunbar's empty mud-hole outpost versus the Utopian Sioux village by the river reveals the deeper moral opposition beneath the surface conflict.

## 4. Natural Settings

Every natural setting carries inherent, near-universal meanings that seem to be part of the human psyche. Truby catalogs them:

- **Ocean**: The surface is an abstract two-dimensional chessboard intensifying the sense of contest. The deep is a three-dimensional weightless world associated with Utopian dreamworlds but also a terrifying graveyard of ancient secrets. (*Moby-Dick*, *Titanic*, *Jaws*, *Finding Nemo*)

- **Outer Space**: The infinite black "ocean of out there," highlighting each thing in its most essential quality. The only remaining natural setting for unlimited adventure. Often paired with myth form. (*2001*, *Star Wars*, *Blade Runner*, *Alien*)

- **Forest**: A natural cathedral of contemplation and lovers' retreat, but also a place where people get lost, where ghosts hide, and mental loss precedes physical death. (*Lord of the Rings*, *Harry Potter*, *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, *The Blair Witch Project*)

- **Jungle**: The state of nature, where suffocation is the primary sensation. Everything grabs you; man is reduced to beast. Expresses the theory of evolution. (*King Kong*, *Heart of Darkness*, *Apocalypse Now*, *Jurassic Park*)

- **Desert and Ice**: Places of dying and death, impersonal in their brutality. The strong-willed go there for toughening through isolation. (*Lawrence of Arabia*, *Fargo*, *The Wild Bunch*, *The Gold Rush*)

- **Island**: A miniature of the earth, a separated laboratory for building and testing new forms of living. Ideal for depicting Utopias, dystopias, and evolution. (*Robinson Crusoe*, *The Tempest*, *Lord of the Flies*, *Jurassic Park*, *Cast Away*)

- **Mountain**: The land of greatness, seclusion, and philosophical insight. Structurally associated with revelation. Negatively, it is the site of hierarchy and tyranny. Always set in opposition to the plain. (*Moses*, *Lost Horizon*, *Batman Begins*, *The Shining*)

- **Plain**: Wide open and accessible, the place of equality and freedom but also mediocrity and herd mentality. Like the ocean surface, its flatness intensifies the sense of contest. (*Shane*, *Dances with Wolves*, *Days of Heaven*)

- **River**: The greatest natural setting for storytelling--a path that is also "the road into or out of somewhere," creating an organic developing line. Going upriver moves toward barbarism (*Heart of Darkness*); going downriver moves toward civilization and love (*The African Queen*). (*Huckleberry Finn*, *Deliverance*, *A River Runs Through It*)

- **Weather**: Lightning and thunder signify passion and death; rain signifies sadness or coziness; wind signifies destruction; fog signifies mystery; sun signifies happiness but also hidden corruption; snow signifies serenity or quiet death. Truby cautions against formulaic use of all natural settings.

## 5. Artificial Spaces

Man-made spaces solve one of the writer's hardest problems: how to express a society. Each is a condenser-expander--a microcosm of hero and society.

- **The House**: The person's first enclosure, shaping the growth of the mind. The warm house is big and diverse, with enough corners for each inhabitant's uniqueness (the "buzzing household" technique of Pieter Brueghel). *Meet Me in St. Louis*, *It's a Wonderful Life*, *Mary Poppins*. The terrifying house has crossed from cocoon to prison--either a Gothic ruin expressing the character's rotted mind (*Great Expectations*, *Wuthering Heights*, *Rebecca*, *Psycho*) or a small, cramped pressure cooker with no escape (*Death of a Salesman*, *A Streetcar Named Desire*, *American Beauty*). Key internal oppositions: cellar (graveyard, dark secrets, criminal plots) versus attic (great thoughts, art, memories, or madness). Safety versus adventure. Ground versus sky.

- **The Bar**: A version of the house. *Cheers* is a Utopian community; *Casablanca*'s Rick's Cafe is simultaneously dystopia (everyone waiting to escape) and Utopia (the perfect community where everyone enjoys their role), creating a Mobius strip world that never stops.

- **The Road**: The opposite of the house. The house calls you to nestle; the road calls you to explore. Myth stories are built on this opposition: the hero leaves an underused home, tests abilities on the road, and returns to realize who he always was.

- **The City**: The largest man-made microcosm. Writers often shrink it to the institution metaphor (highly organized, vast numbers defined by function)--*Network*, *The Matrix*, *Double Indemnity*.

- **Miniatures**: A society shrunk down, showing chaos theory's levels of order. Three uses: letting the audience see the whole world, expressing facets of a character, and showing the exercise of power or tyranny. *Citizen Kane* is built entirely on miniatures (the glass-ball paperweight, the newsreel, Xanadu).

- **Big to Small / Small to Big**: Shrinking a character regresses him to a child, making the mundane sublime and the hero more heroic (must use brain over brawn). Getting big is less interesting because it removes subtlety and plot.

- **Passageways Between Worlds**: A decompression chamber between extremely different subworlds. Tells the audience the rules are about to change. Classic examples: rabbit hole, cyclone, wardrobe, computer screen. Ideally the character moves through slowly; the passageway should be a special world filled with strange, organic inhabitants.

## 6. Technology

Tools are extensions of the human form that magnify capability and become part of a character's identity. Technology is most useful in science fiction, fantasy, and stories placing the hero within a larger social system. In science fiction, invented technology highlights what most troubles the writer about mankind. In fantasy, tools like wands symbolize self-mastery and moral choice. In modernization stories, technology shows societal shifts (*The Magnificent Ambersons* and the automobile; *The Wild Bunch* and the machine gun). Even in drama, mundane tools define character: in *Death of a Salesman*, Willy Loman is always "getting stuck on the machine"--the refrigerator costs $16, the hot water heater costs $97.

## 7. The Vehicle Technique

A major reason journey stories feel fragmented is successive opponents on the road. The vehicle technique solves this: the bigger the vehicle, the more unified the arena, because a large vehicle can bring ongoing opponents along for the ride. The ship in *Titanic* and *Ship of Fools*, the train in *Murder on the Orient Express*, the bus in *Almost Famous*--each creates a contained arena where character conflict can develop continuously rather than episodically.

## 8. Combining Natural Settings with the City

Fantasy opens the city up by imagining it as a natural setting, making the overwhelming city a single recognizable unit while hinting at tremendous potential:

- **City as Mountain**: The highest towers house the most powerful; the poor crawl at the base. Used in stylized crime fantasies like *Batman*.

- **City as Ocean**: Begins on gabled rooftops (floating on waves), then dips below the surface to pick up characters at different levels. The most positive metaphor--characters literally float, ceilings become floors, and people experience ultimate freedom. *Beneath the Rooftops of Paris*, *Mary Poppins* (Mary floats down; chimney sweeps dance on the "sea of enchantment"), *Yellow Submarine*.

- **City as Jungle**: The three-dimensional quality is not liberating but lethal--enemies lurk everywhere. Closely packed, steaming, wet; residents portrayed as animals. *Spider-Man*, *Blade Runner*, *M*, *King Kong*.

- **City as Forest**: The positive version of jungle. Buildings are scaled down, human, like living in trees. A Utopian neighborhood amid impersonal towers. *You Can't Take It with You*, *Ghostbusters* (the firehouse as ultimate tree house for boys).

## 9. Hero's Development and World: Slavery to Freedom Patterns

Truby identifies a one-to-one connection between hero and world. A world is enslaving or freeing based on the balance (or imbalance) of land, people, and technology. He also identifies the "apparent Utopia"--a world that seems perfect but is corrupt beneath the surface (*L.A. Confidential*, *Blue Velvet*). The key question is always: how is the world of slavery an expression of the hero's great weakness?

The complete set of hero/world development patterns:

1. **Slavery to Greater Slavery to Freedom** (both hero and world): *Star Wars* 4-6, *Lord of the Rings*, *The Shawshank Redemption*, *It's a Wonderful Life*

2. **Slavery to Greater Slavery or Death** (both hero and world): *Oedipus*, *Death of a Salesman*, *A Streetcar Named Desire*, *Sunset Boulevard*, *Heart of Darkness*

3. **Hero: Slavery to Greater Slavery/Death; World: Slavery to Greater Slavery to Freedom**: Used in some tragedies where the hero's self-revelation comes too late but his sacrifice sets the world free. *Hamlet*, *The Seven Samurai*, *A Tale of Two Cities*

4. **Slavery to Temporary Freedom to Greater Slavery or Death** (both hero and world): The hero enters a subworld of freedom mid-story--the world where he should live--but fails to realize it in time. *The Wild Bunch*, *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid*, *Dances with Wolves*

5. **Freedom to Slavery or Death** (both hero and world): Begins in a Utopia vulnerable to attack; a flaw or external force causes decline. *King Lear*, *How Green Was My Valley*, *Excalibur*

6. **Freedom to Slavery to Freedom** (both hero and world): Starts free, is attacked, declines, but overcomes the problem to create a stronger Utopia. *Meet Me in St. Louis*, *Amarcord*

7. **Apparent Freedom to Greater Slavery to Freedom** (both hero and world): The world appears Utopian but is corrupt; the hero fights through corruption to create justice. *L.A. Confidential*, *Jurassic Park*, *Blue Velvet*. A brilliant variation in *Goodfellas* combines gangster and black comedy: apparent freedom leads to greater slavery and death for all.

## 10. Time in Story World

Time is the fourth major building block alongside natural settings, man-made spaces, and tools. Truby first dispels two fallacies:

- **Fallacy of the Past**: Historical fiction is not about depicting another world's values non-judgmentally. You use the past as glasses through which the audience sees itself more clearly today--either negatively (past values that still hurt) or positively (past values worth reviving).

- **Fallacy of the Future**: Science fiction is not about predicting the future. Stories set in the future abstract the present to highlight forces, choices, and consequences we face today.

True "natural" time techniques include:

- **Seasons**: Placing the story within a particular season conveys meaning. Covering all four creates a circular story emphasizing how things remain the same. The classic connection: summer (freedom/vulnerability), fall (decline), winter (lowest point), spring (rise). Cutting against this sequence asserts that humans are not enslaved by natural patterns. *Meet Me in St. Louis* and *Amarcord* execute this beautifully.

- **Holidays and Rituals**: A ritual is a philosophy translated into repeated actions--already a dramatic event with strong visual elements. The writer must examine and decide whether to support or attack the ritual's philosophy. Jean Shepherd's *A Christmas Story* uses the holiday as a time passageway to childhood Utopia. *Hannah and Her Sisters* uses three Thanksgivings to structure a movement from fractured community to real community.

- **The Single Day**: Creates simultaneous story movement while maintaining narrative drive. A twelve-hour clock creates a funnel of urgency (*American Graffiti*). A twenty-four-hour clock increases circularity--most things remain the same, making the few changes more significant (*Ulysses*, *Groundhog Day*). The "perfect day" variation connects communal activity to a natural time increment to create Utopian harmony (*Witness* and the barn-raising).

- **Time Endpoint (Ticking Clock)**: The audience is told action must complete by a specific time. Provides intense narrative drive at the expense of texture. Creates the fastest funnel, allowing hundreds of characters to act simultaneously with urgency. Most common in action, thriller, and caper stories (*Speed*, *Ocean's Eleven*). Also effective in comic journey stories, where it gives a forward line through meandering episodes (*The Blues Brothers*).

## 11. Visual Seven Steps

Each of the seven key story structure steps tends to have its own unique visual subworld within the overall arena. This is the technique for creating total orchestration of story and world:

1. **Weakness and Need**: A subworld that is a physical manifestation of the hero's weakness or fear. In *Star Wars*, Luke's barren desert. In *It's a Wonderful Life*, the God's-eye view of the town and night sky.

2. **Desire**: A subworld in which the hero expresses his goal. In *It's a Wonderful Life*, George's warm childhood house contrasted with the deserted house he throws a rock at.

3. **Opponent**: A unique place expressing the opponent's power and ability to attack the hero's great weakness--an extreme version of the hero's world of slavery. The Death Star in *Star Wars*. Potter's bank in *It's a Wonderful Life*. Norma Desmond's run-down mansion in *Sunset Boulevard*.

4. **Apparent Defeat or Temporary Freedom**: The narrowest space in the story to that point, with all forces pressing in. In *Star Wars*, the collapsing garbage dump. In *The Wild Bunch*, the bridge with lit dynamite. In rare stories where the hero ends enslaved, this is replaced by temporary freedom in a Utopian subworld (the Mexican village under trees in *The Wild Bunch*).

5. **Visit to Death**: The underworld or a place representing decline, aging, and death. In *It's a Wonderful Life*, the dystopian Pottersville ending at the graveyard. In *Star Wars*, this is combined with apparent defeat in the monster-filled waters of the garbage dump.

6. **Battle**: The most confined place of the entire story, creating a pressure-cooker effect. In *Star Wars*, the narrow trench. In *The Wild Bunch*, the coliseum packed with hundreds of opponents. In *Sunset Boulevard*, the pool where Norma shoots Joe. In *Harry Potter*, the enclosed underground room ringed with fire.

7. **Freedom or Slavery**: The world's endpoint expressed in physical terms. In *Star Wars*, the Hall of Heroes. In *It's a Wonderful Life*, the warm house filled with grateful friends. In *The Wild Bunch*, the wind-blown ghost town. In *Meet Me in St. Louis*, the reborn warm house and the World's Fair as temporary Utopia.

Truby demonstrates these principles exhaustively through detailed analyses of *Star Wars*, *The Wild Bunch*, *Meet Me in St. Louis*, *It's a Wonderful Life*, *Sunset Boulevard*, *Ulysses*, and *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone*--each showing how natural settings, man-made spaces, technology, and time are orchestrated with the seven structure steps to create a total fusion of character and world.
