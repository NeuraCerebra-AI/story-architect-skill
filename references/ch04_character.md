# Chapter 4: Character -- Analytical Summary

## Character Web: How Characters Connect Through Story Function

Truby's central thesis on character is that writers must never conceive of characters as isolated individuals. Every character exists within a **character web**, a network of relationships where each person helps define the others through comparison and contrast. A character is often defined by who he is *not*.

Characters connect in four major ways: **story function**, **archetype**, **theme**, and **opposition**. The key story functions are:

- **Hero**: The main character who drives the action, possesses the central problem, and pursues a goal (desire) while struggling with weaknesses and needs.
- **Opponent**: The character who most wants to prevent the hero from achieving his desire. Crucially, the opponent should want the *same thing* as the hero, forcing direct conflict. The opponent is not merely a block but a structural mirror -- the relationship between hero and opponent is the single most important relationship in the story.
- **Ally**: The hero's helper and sounding board, allowing the audience to hear the hero's values and feelings.
- **Fake-Ally Opponent**: Appears to be the hero's friend but is actually an opponent. One of the most complex and fascinating characters because he is torn by a dilemma -- while pretending to be an ally, he sometimes genuinely becomes one.
- **Fake-Opponent Ally**: Appears to fight the hero but is actually a friend. Less common and less useful than the fake-ally opponent because plot comes primarily from opposition.
- **Subplot Character**: Contrasts how the hero and a second character deal with the same problem in slightly different ways. The subplot character is usually *not* the ally; he tracks a parallel line with a different result.

## Four Requirements of a Great Hero

1. **Make your lead character constantly fascinating.** There must be no dead time. One effective method is making the character mysterious -- showing the audience the character is hiding something, which forces active participation.

2. **Make the audience identify with the character, but not too much.** Audiences identify based on the hero's *desire* and *moral problem* (need), not superficial traits like background, job, or demographics. However, too much identification prevents the audience from stepping back to observe the hero's growth.

3. **Make the audience empathize with your hero, not sympathize.** Empathy (understanding and caring) matters more than likability. The key to maintaining audience interest even when a character acts immorally is to always show the hero's *motive*. Showing why the hero acts as he does creates empathy without requiring approval.

4. **Give your hero a moral as well as a psychological need.** A psychological need affects only the hero; a moral need concerns learning to act properly toward others. Having both increases the character's effect and the story's emotional power.

## Character Change: How to Determine and Structure It

Character change (also called character arc or range of change) is potentially the most difficult and most important step in writing. Truby rejects the "light switch school" of character change -- flipping a switch in the last scene. Instead, character change is made possible at the *beginning* by how the writer sets it up.

The hero must be conceived as a **range of change**, a range of possibilities, from the very start. The range is defined by the character's understanding of himself. True character change involves a **challenging and changing of basic beliefs, leading to new moral action**. External changes (poor to rich, peasant to king) are not character changes unless they involve this deeper transformation.

The essential technique: **Begin at the end of the change** -- write the self-revelation first, then go back to determine the starting point (need and desire), then figure out the steps of development in between. This guarantees that every step leads to the destination, making the story organic.

Common types of character change include:

- **Child to Adult**: Challenging basic beliefs and taking new moral action (e.g., *Huckleberry Finn*, *The Catcher in the Rye*, *Good Will Hunting*).
- **Adult to Leader**: Moving from self-concern to helping others find the right path (e.g., *The Matrix*, *Schindler's List*, *Hamlet*).
- **Cynic to Participant**: A specialized form of adult-to-leader where the character moves from selfish isolation to rejoining society (e.g., *Casablanca*, Han Solo in *Star Wars*).
- **Leader to Tyrant**: Negative change from helping others to forcing others to follow (e.g., *The Godfather*, *Macbeth*, *A Few Good Men*).
- **Leader to Visionary**: Seeing how an entire society should change. The writer must provide a detailed moral vision, not just imagery (e.g., Moses, *Close Encounters*).
- **Metamorphosis**: Extreme change where the character becomes another person, animal, or thing. Implies a weak, fractured, or devastated initial self (e.g., *The Fly*, *Kafka's Metamorphosis*).

## Archetypes: Strengths and Weaknesses

Archetypes are fundamental psychological patterns and social roles with universal appeal. The **shadow** is the negative tendency of each archetype. Writers must always make archetypes specific and individual to avoid stereotypes.

**King or Father** -- *Strength*: Leads family or people with wisdom, foresight, and resolve. *Weaknesses*: Can force oppressive rules, remove himself from the emotional realm, or insist others live solely for his benefit. (Examples: King Arthur, *The Godfather*, *Citizen Kane*.)

**Queen or Mother** -- *Strength*: Provides the care and protective shell for growth. *Weaknesses*: Can become protective or controlling to the point of tyranny, or use guilt and shame to hold the child close. (Examples: *Macbeth*, *The Glass Menagerie*.)

**Wise Old Man / Wise Old Woman / Mentor / Teacher** -- *Strength*: Passes on knowledge and wisdom for better lives and societal improvement. *Weaknesses*: Can force students to think a certain way or speak for personal glory rather than the glory of ideas. (Examples: Yoda, Hannibal Lecter as mentor, Gandalf.)

**Warrior** -- *Strength*: The practical enforcer of what is right. *Weaknesses*: Can live by "kill or be killed," believing whatever is weak must be destroyed, becoming the enforcer of what is wrong. (Examples: Achilles, Luke Skywalker, *Seven Samurai*.)

**Magician or Shaman** -- *Strength*: Makes visible the deeper reality behind the senses; balances hidden forces of the natural world. *Weakness*: Can manipulate deeper reality to enslave others and destroy the natural order. (Examples: *Harry Potter*, Sherlock Holmes, *Chinatown*.)

**Trickster** -- *Strength*: Uses confidence, trickery, and words to get what he wants. *Weakness*: May become a complete liar who looks out only for himself. (Examples: Odysseus, Indiana Jones, *Catch Me If You Can*.)

**Artist or Clown** -- *Strengths*: Defines excellence, shows beauty and a vision of the future, or reveals what is ugly or foolish. *Weaknesses*: Can be the ultimate fascist insisting on perfection, may create a controlled special world, or tears everything down. (Examples: Blanche in *Streetcar*, Hamlet, Stephen in *Ulysses*.)

**Lover** -- *Strength*: Provides care, understanding, and sensuality that make someone complete. *Weaknesses*: Can lose himself in the other or force the other to stand in his shadow. (Examples: Heathcliff, Romeo and Juliet, Rick and Ilsa in *Casablanca*.)

**Rebel** -- *Strength*: Courage to stand against an enslaving system. *Weakness*: Often cannot provide a better alternative, ending up only destroying the system. (Examples: Prometheus, *Rebel Without a Cause*, Rick in *Casablanca*.)

## Four-Corner Opposition

Four-corner opposition moves beyond a simple hero-versus-opponent dynamic by creating a **hero, a main opponent, and at least two secondary opponents**, each occupying a "corner" and being as different from the others as possible. Five rules govern its use:

1. **Each opponent uses a different way of attacking the hero's great weakness.** This guarantees all conflict is organically connected to the hero's central flaw while representing a miniature society.
2. **Place each character in conflict not only with the hero but with every other character.** This exponentially increases available conflict and creates a dense plot.
3. **Put the values of all four characters in conflict.** Each character expresses a unique system of values and way of life that collides with the other three, providing tremendous thematic texture.
4. **Push the characters to the corners.** Make each as different as possible from the other three, focusing on how their values differ. Identify positive and negative versions of the same value (e.g., determined/aggressive, honest/insensitive).
5. **Extend the four-corner pattern to every level of the story** -- society, institution, family, even within a single character.

The *Streetcar Named Desire* example illustrates this: Blanche (artist), Stanley (warrior-king), Stella (mother), and Mitch each occupy a corner with distinct weaknesses, needs, desires, and values, all circling the central moral problem of whether lies and illusion are ever justified in pursuit of love.

## Double Reversal

The double reversal is an advanced technique where **both the hero and the opponent receive a self-revelation**. Each learns from the other, giving the audience two insights about how to act and live instead of one. The author's moral vision becomes the *best of what both characters learn*.

Steps to create a double reversal:

1. Give both hero and main opponent a weakness and a need (they need not be the same).
2. Make the opponent human -- capable of learning and changing.
3. During or just after the battle, give both a self-revelation.
4. Connect the two revelations: the hero learns something from the opponent and vice versa.
5. The author's moral vision is the synthesis of both characters' discoveries.

The technique is uncommon because most writers create opponents incapable of self-revelation (purely evil opponents cannot learn). It appears most frequently in love stories, where hero and lover learn from each other (e.g., *Pride and Prejudice*, *Kramer vs. Kramer*, *Casablanca*, *The Music Man*).

## Two Main Characters: Love Stories and Buddy Stories

**Love Stories**: Designed to show the value of a community between two equals. The central idea is that a person becomes a unique individual only by entering a community of two. Practically, one character must be slightly more central, holding the main desire line. The lover functions as the main *opponent*, not a second hero. Outside opponents (family members, rival suitors) fill out the web for comparative contrast.

**Buddy Stories**: The hero is essentially cut in two, showing two approaches to life and two sets of talents. One buddy (usually the thinker or strategist) is more central. The buddy functions as both first opponent and first ally; opposition between them takes the form of good-natured bickering. Outside dangerous opponents round out the web, and because buddy stories often use a mythic journey, secondary opponents encountered on the road each represent a negative aspect of society. A fundamental snag in the relationship provides ongoing internal opposition.

## Multiple Heroes and Narrative Drive

Stories with multiple heroes create simultaneous story movement but risk losing forward narrative drive. Each main character must pass through all seven structural steps (weakness/need, desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation, new equilibrium). Techniques to maintain drive include:

- Have one character emerge as more central over the course of the story.
- Give all characters the same desire line.
- Make the hero of one storyline the opponent in another.
- Connect characters through a single subject or theme.
- Use cliffhangers to trigger jumps between storylines.
- Funnel characters from many locations into one.
- Compress time (one day, one night).
- Show the same holiday or group event at least three times to indicate forward movement.
- Have characters occasionally meet by coincidence.

## Individualizing Characters Through Theme and Opposition

After establishing story functions and archetypes, characters become real individuals through comparison based on **theme** (the author's moral vision) and **opposition**. The process:

1. Write down the central moral problem of the story.
2. Compare hero and all other characters on: weaknesses, psychological and moral needs, desire, values, power/status/ability, and how each faces the central moral problem.
3. Begin with the hero-versus-main-opponent comparison, then compare the hero to other opponents and allies, then compare opponents and allies to one another.
4. Each character should represent a *different approach* to the hero's central moral problem -- variations on a theme.

The *Tootsie* example is instructive: the central moral problem is "how a man treats a woman," and every character (Ron, Julie, John, Sandy, Les, Rita, Michael-as-Dorothy versus Michael-as-man) embodies a distinct variation on that problem.

## Comparing Characters: The Systematic Technique

The systematic comparison technique is the practical method that underlies all character creation. For each character, the writer lists and compares:

1. **Weaknesses** -- what holds each character back.
2. **Need** -- both psychological (affects only the character) and moral (affects others).
3. **Desire** -- the specific goal each pursues.
4. **Values** -- the cluster of beliefs about what makes life good (including positive and negative versions of the same value).
5. **Power, status, and ability** -- each character's position and capabilities.
6. **How each faces the central moral problem** -- the unique approach and justification each character uses.

The comparison always begins between hero and main opponent, extends outward to secondary opponents and allies, and finally compares opponents and allies to each other. Minor characters should also function as variations on the hero's weakness and moral problem. This rigorous comparative method ensures that no character exists in isolation and that every character serves the organic whole of the story.
