# Chapter 8: Plot -- Creating an Organic Plot

## Analytical Summary

Plot is not a sequence of events imposed from outside. It is the organic expression of the hero's attempt to solve a deep life problem, growing directly from character, moral argument, story world, and the web of opposition. This chapter lays out a complete system for building plot through the twenty-two structure steps, supplemented by advanced techniques including subplot, double reversal, thematic revelation, and the management of revelations as the primary engine of dramatic momentum.

---

## 1. Organic Plot vs. Mechanical Plot

An organic plot is one in which every event grows necessarily from character and conflict. The sequence of actions feels inevitable because each step emerges from the hero's desire, the opponent's counterattack, and the revelations that force course corrections. A mechanical plot, by contrast, imposes arbitrary events on the characters from outside -- things happen because the writer needs them to, not because the characters' choices demand them.

The method for creating an organic plot follows a specific sequence: decide whether to use a storyteller (which affects how information reaches the audience), work through the twenty-two structure steps in detail to generate most plot beats, and then layer in genre-specific beats while twisting them to avoid predictability. The twenty-two steps are not a formula. They are scaffolding that increases creativity by guaranteeing the story unfolds organically. They combine the character web, the moral argument, the story world, and the actual event sequence into a single choreography of hero versus opponents.

The number twenty-two is flexible. A thirty-second commercial can work with the seven essential steps. A feature film typically hits all twenty-two. A novel like David Copperfield may have sixty or more revelations. The system is an accordion -- it can only contract so far (no fewer than seven steps), but it can expand as far as the story demands.

---

## 2. The Twenty-Two Steps

### Step 1: Self-Revelation, Need, and Desire
The framing step. Start by determining what the hero will learn at the end (self-revelation), then work backward to establish what he knows and believes at the beginning, what he is wrong about, and what he wants. This frame establishes the structural journey and ensures every subsequent step leads to the endpoint. Three questions govern this step: What will my hero learn? What does he know at the beginning? What is he wrong about?

### Step 2: Ghost and Story World
**Ghost:** Not generic backstory but the specific event from the past that still haunts the hero in the present. The ghost is an open wound, often the source of the hero's psychological and moral weakness. It functions as an internal opponent and a counter-desire -- the hero's desire drives him forward while the ghost holds him back. Ibsen called it "sailing with a corpse in the cargo." The ghost should not be over-explained at the start; withholding details draws the audience toward the story. A second, rarer kind of ghost applies to heroes who begin in a paradise world (no haunting event), where the attack comes from outside.

**Story World:** Present from page one, the world should be an expression of the hero -- showing his weaknesses, needs, desires, and obstacles. If the hero begins enslaved, the world should be enslaving and should exacerbate his great weakness.

### Step 3: Weakness and Need
The hero has one or more flaws so serious they are ruining his life. Weaknesses are always psychological (inner damage); they are also moral when they cause someone else to get hurt. The key test for moral weakness: is the hero clearly hurting at least one other person at the beginning? Need is what the hero must fulfill to have a better life, almost always requiring him to overcome his weaknesses by the end. The problem is the immediate crisis the hero faces -- an outgrowth of weakness, designed to show that weakness to the audience quickly.

### Step 4: Inciting Event
An external event that jolts the hero out of paralysis and forces action. It connects need and desire. The guiding principle is "from the frying pan into the fire": the best inciting event makes the hero think he has just overcome his crisis, when in fact he has fallen into the worst trouble of his life. In Sunset Boulevard, Joe's blown tire leads him into Norma Desmond's driveway -- apparent escape, actual trap.

### Step 5: Desire
The hero's specific goal, providing the spine of the entire plot. Three principles: it should be specific, extend through most of the story, and start at a low level so it can build. As the story progresses, increase the intensity and stakes of the original desire rather than introducing an entirely new one. Desire lines range from low (survival/escape, which reduces the hero to animal level) to high (ideological or spiritual).

### Step 6: Ally or Allies
Characters who help the hero overcome the opponent. An ally should ideally have his own desire line (the Scarecrow wants a brain). The ally must never be more interesting than the hero -- if he is, redesign the story so the ally becomes the hero.

### Step 7: Opponent and/or Mystery
The character who wants to prevent the hero from reaching his goal -- the most important relationship in the story. The best opponent is the necessary one: the character best able to attack the hero's great weakness. Opponent and mystery are related for two reasons: a mysterious opponent is harder to defeat (giving the hero a two-part task of uncovering then defeating), and in detective/thriller stories, mystery compensates for a hidden opponent. Key questions: Who wants to stop the hero and why? What does the opponent want (should compete for the same goal)? What are the opponent's values and how do they differ?

### Step 8: Fake-Ally Opponent
A character who appears to be the hero's ally but is actually an opponent or works for the main opponent. This increases the opponent's power because its true nature is hidden. The fake-ally opponent is inherently complex -- by pretending to be an ally, the character often starts genuinely feeling like one, creating an internal dilemma. In Casablanca, Captain Renault works for the Nazis while remaining charming toward Rick, then flips to become a true ally at the climax.

### Step 9: First Revelation and Decision
The hero receives a surprising piece of new information that forces a decision, a new direction, and adjustments to desire and motive. All four events -- revelation, decision, changed desire, changed motive -- should occur simultaneously. The quality of the plot comes down to the quality of the revelations. Each must be explosive and progressively stronger. Hit films average seven to ten major reveals; detective stories and thrillers have more. The reliance on three-act structure (two or three plot points) produces a weak plot with no chance of competing professionally.

### Step 10: Plan
The set of guidelines and strategies the hero will use to overcome the opponent. The hero's initial plan should almost always fail because the opponent is too strong. This forces the hero to dig deeper and devise a better strategy. A plan that simply plays out produces a predictable plot and a superficial hero.

### Step 11: Opponent's Plan and Main Counterattack
The opponent also has a strategy and executes attacks against the hero. The more intricate the opponent's plan and the better it is hidden, the better the plot. Each hidden attack, when sprung, becomes another reveal.

### Step 12: Drive
The largest section of the plot -- the series of actions the hero takes to defeat the opponent, running from the plan through the apparent defeat. During the drive, the opponent is usually too strong, so the hero is losing and becomes desperate, often taking immoral steps to win (part of the moral argument). The key requirement: plot development, not repetition. The hero must react to new information about the opponent and adjust accordingly. Four different activities that are the same plot beat (falling in love through beach, movies, park, dinner) constitute repetition, not development.

### Step 13: Attack by Ally
When the hero starts taking immoral steps to win, the ally confronts him, becoming the hero's conscience. This creates a second level of conflict (the first being hero vs. opponent) and increases pressure on the hero, forcing him to begin questioning his values. The hero typically does not accept the criticism.

### Step 14: Apparent Defeat
Two-thirds to three-quarters through the story, the hero suffers what seems like total defeat -- his lowest point. This is not a small setback; it must be explosive and devastating. The audience must genuinely believe the hero is finished. There should be only one apparent defeat to maintain dramatic shape. In stories ending in the hero's destruction, this step becomes an apparent victory -- the hero reaches peak success, but everything deteriorates from here (e.g., the Lufthansa heist in Goodfellas).

### Step 15: Second Revelation and Decision
Just after the apparent defeat, the hero receives new information showing victory is still possible. This galvanizes him -- desire becomes obsession. He will do virtually anything to win, becoming tyrannical in his quest and continuing his moral decline. All five elements must occur: revelation, decision, obsessive drive, changed desire, changed motive.

### Step 16: Audience Revelation
The audience -- but not the hero -- learns an important piece of information, often the true identity of the fake-ally opponent. This shifts the hero-audience relationship: for the first time, the audience knows more than the hero, creating distance that allows the audience to step back and observe the hero's overall process of change culminating in self-revelation.

### Step 17: Third Revelation and Decision
The hero learns what the audience discovered in Step 16. Rather than causing retreat, this knowledge makes the hero feel stronger and more determined because he can now see everything he is up against.

### Step 18: Gate, Gauntlet, Visit to Death
Conflict intensifies to nearly unbearable pressure. Options narrow, space contracts. The hero must pass through a narrow gate or long gauntlet. Psychologically, the hero confronts his own mortality -- life is finite and could end soon. Rather than causing flight, this realization spurs the hero to fight: "If my life is to have meaning, I must take a stand here and now." This step is the most movable of the twenty-two and can appear at the apparent defeat, during the battle, or after it.

### Step 19: Battle
The final conflict determining who wins the goal. Everything converges -- all characters and action lines meet in the smallest possible space. A big, violent conflict is the least interesting form; the emphasis should be on which ideas or values win, not which force is superior. The battle is where the hero usually fulfills his need and gains his desire, where he is most like his main opponent (with crucial differences sharpened), and where theme first explodes in the audience's mind.

### Step 20: Self-Revelation
Through the crucible of battle, the hero learns who he really is, tearing aside the facade he has lived behind. If also moral, he learns the proper way to act toward others. A great self-revelation should be sudden, shattering, and genuinely new to the hero. Two pitfalls: what the hero learns must be truly meaningful (not platitudes), and the hero should not directly state his lesson to the audience.

### Step 21: Moral Decision
The hero chooses between two courses of action, each representing a set of values and a way of living that affects others. This is the proof of the self-revelation -- action demonstrating what the hero has become.

### Step 22: New Equilibrium
Everything returns to normal, but with one crucial difference: the hero is now at either a higher or lower level because of his self-revelation.

## 3. Opening Types

Three structural openings establish ghost, story world, weakness, need, and problem:

- **Community Start:** The hero lives in a paradise world of harmony. No ghost. He is happy but vulnerable to attack, which will come soon from without or within. Examples: Meet Me in St. Louis, The Deer Hunter.
- **Running Start:** The classic opening designed to catch the reader immediately. The hero has a strong ghost, lives in a world of slavery, has serious weaknesses, both psychological and moral needs, and faces one or more problems. Most good stories use this opening.
- **Slow Start:** A purposeless hero who must discover his true desire (his self-revelation). The first three-quarters of the story have no goal and no narrative drive. Extremely difficult to execute; On the Waterfront and Rebel Without a Cause are rare successes.

---

## 4. Plot Technique: Subplot

A subplot compares how the hero and a second character deal with generally the same situation. Two strict rules govern it: the subplot must affect the hero's main plot (if it does not, it creates two simultaneous stories that make the main plot drag), and the subplot character is usually not the ally (they serve different functions). In Hamlet, subplot character Laertes allies with main opponent Claudius and duels Hamlet in the battle scene, tying the subplot directly into the main plot.

Subplot improves character, theme, and texture but slows narrative drive. If used, only the seven key steps can be covered. It should be introduced as early as is naturally appropriate.

---

## 5. Plot Technique: Double Reversal

At the self-revelation step, both the hero and the opponent undergo change. Each learns from the other, giving the audience two insights about how to act and live. The method: give both hero and opponent a weakness and need; make the opponent human (capable of learning); give both a self-revelation during or after the battle; connect the two revelations so each learns from the other; and express the author's moral vision as the best of what both characters learn. In Casablanca, Rick regains his idealism while Renault announces he has become a patriot, joining Rick on his new path.

---

## 6. Plot Technique: Thematic Revelation

A revelation gained not by the hero but by the audience -- showing how people in general should act and live, allowing the story to grow beyond its particular characters. The key is drawing the abstract and general from the real and specific, finding a particular gesture or action with symbolic impact. In Places in the Heart, the final communion scene gradually evolves from realistic depiction into universal forgiveness: the banker, the driven-away black man, the dead sheriff, and the boy who killed him all drink the wine and say "Peace of God." The technique should not be avoided out of fear of sounding pretentious.

---

## 7. Revelations as the Engine of Plot

Revelations are the keys to plot -- the single most important technique for building dramatic momentum. Three principles govern their sequencing: revelations must be logical (occurring in the order the hero would most likely discover them), they must build in intensity (each ideally stronger than the last), and they must come at an increasing pace (greater density of surprise heightens drama). The most powerful form is the reversal -- a reveal that turns the audience's understanding of everything on its head (The Sixth Sense, The Usual Suspects). The reversal is devastating when placed at the very end but risks reducing the story to a mere vehicle for plot if not handled carefully.

---

## 8. The Iceberg Opponent

The opponent should be treated as an iceberg -- most of the danger is hidden below the surface. Four techniques make the opposition maximally dangerous: create a hierarchy of opponents with alliances (the main opponent at the pyramid's top), hide the hierarchy and each opponent's true agenda from hero and audience, reveal this information in pieces at increasing pace (more reveals near the end), and have the hero face an obvious opponent early while discovering stronger hidden opposition as conflict intensifies.

---

## Key Principles for Application

The twenty-two steps represent the organic order by which humans solve life problems. Flexibility is essential -- every good story works through the steps in a slightly different order. But changing the order too drastically risks a story that feels fake or contrived, because these steps mirror real human problem-solving processes. The system requires extensive practice; it should be applied to everything the writer reads and writes. The twenty-two steps guarantee that the main character drives the plot, producing stories that are simultaneously structured and alive.
