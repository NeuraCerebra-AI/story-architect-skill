# Chapter 2: Premise -- Analytical Summary

## 1. Core Concepts: What a Premise Is and Why It Matters

A **premise** is the entire story stated in one sentence. It combines the starting event, a sense of the main character, and a sense of the outcome. Examples: *The Godfather* -- "The youngest son of a Mafia family takes revenge on the men who shot his father and becomes the new Godfather"; *Star Wars* -- "When a princess falls into mortal danger, a young man uses his skills as a fighter to save her and defeat the evil forces of a galactic empire."

The premise matters for four reasons. First, it serves as a marketable "high concept" -- a one-line hook that audiences grasp instantly. Second, it is the writer's inspiration, the excitement that sustains months or years of work. Third, it is the writer's prison: choosing one idea forecloses thousands of others, so the choice must be sound. Fourth, and most critically, every subsequent decision -- character, plot, theme, symbol, dialogue -- flows from the premise. A flawed premise cannot be rescued by excellence anywhere else. Truby estimates that nine out of ten writers fail at the premise stage, usually because they do not know how to develop the idea and extract its deeper potential.

A high-concept premise, by itself, yields only two or three scenes (those surrounding the central twist). A feature film requires forty to seventy scenes; a novel may need double that. Only full craft can bridge that gap.

## 2. The 10-Step Premise Technique

### Step 1: Write Something That May Change Your Life

Begin with self-exploration. Create a **wish list** (everything you would love to see on screen or page -- characters, twists, dialogue, themes, genres) and a **premise list** (every story idea you have ever had, each expressed in one sentence). Lay both lists side by side and look for recurring patterns -- character types, voice qualities, genres, themes, time periods. These patterns reveal your raw creative vision. A premise rooted in genuine personal stakes is far more likely to be original and compelling.

### Step 2: Look for What Is Possible

Do not lock onto the first promising direction. Brainstorm multiple paths the idea could take. Two techniques help: (a) identify what the idea **promises** -- the expectations an audience will bring -- and (b) repeatedly ask **"What if...?"** to push the idea beyond the obvious. The "what if" question simultaneously defines the boundaries of the story world and explores the writer's imagination within it. Examples: *Tootsie* -- what if you make the hero a chauvinist forced into the one disguise he least wants but most needs? *Chinatown* -- what if the crime keeps getting bigger, from adultery to the founding corruption of an entire city? *The Godfather* -- what if you elevate a crime boss to a Shakespearean king and turn a mob story into a dark American epic?

### Step 3: Identify Story Challenges and Problems

Every idea carries unique structural problems embedded in its DNA. These must be confronted at the premise stage, not after a full draft. Identifying challenges early converts obstacles into creative signposts. Examples: *Star Wars* must introduce many characters across vast space/time and create change in a hero who is morally good from the start. *Forrest Gump* must turn forty years of history into one cohesive personal story driven by a mentally challenged protagonist. *The Great Gatsby* must create narrative drive when the hero is someone else's helper and must make the audience care about shallow people.

### Step 5: Determine Your Best Character

"Best" means the most fascinating, challenging, and complex character -- not necessarily the nicest. Ask: "Who do I love? Do I want to watch him act? Do I love the way he thinks? Do I care about his challenges?" If the best character is not currently the protagonist, restructure the premise immediately. In multi-protagonist stories, find the best character for each storyline.

### Step 6: Get a Sense of the Central Conflict

Reduce the story to one line by answering: **"Who fights whom over what?"** All conflict in the story will boil down to this single issue. Keep this statement, alongside the designing principle, visible throughout the writing process.

### Step 7: Get a Sense of the Single Cause-and-Effect Pathway

Every organic story has one spine: A leads to B leads to C and so on to Z. A split premise (two unconnected causal lines) must be unified. Find the spine by asking: **"What is my hero's basic action?"** -- the single action that unifies every other action the hero takes. In *Star Wars*, it is "uses his skills as a fighter." In *The Godfather*, it is "takes revenge." Multi-hero stories need one spine per storyline, with all spines converging into a larger encompassing spine (as in *The Canterbury Tales*).

## 3. The Designing Principle

The designing principle is the internal logic that organizes the story as a whole, making the parts hang together organically so the story becomes greater than the sum of its parts. Stated as a formula:

> **Designing Principle = Story Process + Original Execution**

It differs from the premise: the premise is concrete (what actually happens); the designing principle is abstract (the deeper process, told in an original way). Most stories lack a designing principle and therefore remain generic. Finding one is what makes a story original.

**How to find it:** Do not impose a genre template. Instead, "induce" the form from the premise itself, like a detective. Techniques include using a journey or traveling metaphor (Huck Finn's raft trip, Marlow's river journey in *Heart of Darkness*, Alice's fall down the rabbit hole) or anchoring to a central symbol (the red letter A in *The Scarlet Letter*, the whale in *Moby-Dick*). Other organizing devices include units of time (a single day, four seasons), a unique storyteller structure, or a special mode of unfolding.

**Key examples of Premise vs. Designing Principle:**
- *The Godfather* -- Premise: youngest son takes revenge and becomes the new Godfather. Designing Principle: use the classic fairy-tale strategy of the youngest of three sons becoming the new "king."
- *Tootsie* -- Premise: actor disguises himself as a woman for a TV role. Designing Principle: force a male chauvinist to live as a woman.
- *It's a Wonderful Life* -- Premise: angel shows a suicidal man what the world would be without him. Designing Principle: express the power of the individual by showing what a town and a nation would be like if one man had never lived.
- *Citizen Kane* -- Premise: tell the life of a rich newspaper baron. Designing Principle: use multiple storytellers to show that a man's life can never be fully known.
- *Harry Potter* -- Premise: boy discovers magical powers and attends a school for magicians. Designing Principle: a magician prince learns to be a man and a king over the course of seven school years at a boarding school for sorcerers.

## 4. Character Change Formula: The W-A-C Technique

Character change is expressed as a three-part equation (not to be confused with three-act structure):

> **W x A = C**

- **W** = the hero's weaknesses (psychological and moral) at the beginning.
- **A** = the basic action -- the central struggle in the middle of the story (the fulcrum).
- **C** = the changed person at the end (positive or negative transformation).

The method works as follows:

1. Write the one-line premise.
2. Identify the hero's basic action (A).
3. Go to the **opposites** of A to determine both W (starting weaknesses) and C (end state).

Going to opposites is essential: if the hero's initial traits resemble the basic action, no change can occur -- the character merely deepens existing qualities. The basic action should be the one action best able to force the character to confront his weaknesses and change.

**Star Wars:** A = "uses his skills as a fighter." W (opposites of a skilled fighter) = naive, impetuous, paralyzed, unfocused, lacking confidence. C = self-esteem, a place among the chosen few, a fighter for good.

**The Godfather:** A = "takes revenge." W (opposites of vengeful) = unconcerned, afraid, mainstream, legitimate, separated from the family. C = tyrannical, absolute ruler of the family. If Michael had started vengeful, revenge would produce no change -- only more of the same.

Writers should generate multiple W and C options for each premise and remain open to revising the character change throughout development.

## 5. Story Challenges: Identification and Resolution

Story challenges are structural problems unique to a given idea that cannot be escaped and must be solved for successful execution. The technique is to surface these problems at the premise stage rather than after a complete draft, when they feel set in concrete. Writers should list every inherent difficulty they can identify.

Illustrative challenges from the source material: *Beloved* must avoid portraying the hero as a victim while maintaining narrative drive across constant time jumps and making distant events feel present. *Jaws* must create a fair fight with a limited-intelligence opponent, enable frequent attacks, and end with a one-on-one confrontation. *Huckleberry Finn* must show the immoral fabric of an entire nation through a boy's eyes while maintaining momentum in an episodic, traveling structure. *Death of a Salesman* must turn a small man's life into grand tragedy while mixing past and present without confusion.

---

## 6. Moral Choice

Theme is best expressed through a **moral argument** -- the case for how to live, made through character actions rather than philosophical statement. The most important step in that argument is the hero's final moral choice.

A **fake choice** is between a positive and a negative (prison vs. winning the girl) -- the outcome is obvious and dramatically inert. A **true moral choice** forces the hero to select between two positive outcomes (or, rarely, to avoid one of two negatives, as in *Sophie's Choice*). The options should be as equal as possible, with one seeming only slightly better. The classic dilemma is love vs. honor: in *A Farewell to Arms* the hero chooses love; in *The Maltese Falcon* the hero chooses honor. In the *Tootsie* worked example, Michael sacrifices his lucrative acting job and apologizes to Julie for lying -- choosing honesty and love over career success.

---

## 7. Audience Appeal

The final test: **"Is this single storyline unique enough to interest a lot of people besides me?"** Writers must answer ruthlessly. If only the writer and immediate family would care, the premise should be abandoned. The either/or trap -- "write what I care about OR write what sells" -- is a false distinction. Writers will generate more ideas in a lifetime than they can develop; they should always seek the intersection of personal meaning and broad appeal. Writing for an audience makes it easier to sustain the work you love.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Tootsie (Full Walkthrough)
- **Premise:** Actor cannot get work, disguises himself as a woman, gets a TV role, falls in love with a female cast member.
- **Possibilities:** Funny look at modern dating plus deep examination of the immorality underlying how men and women treat each other.
- **Story Challenges:** Show the effect of men's immoral actions without attacking one entire gender while making the other look innocent.
- **Problems:** Make a man believable as a woman; weave several man-woman plots into one; end each plotline successfully; create an emotionally satisfying love story while using farce techniques.
- **Designing Principle:** Force a male chauvinist to live as a woman. Place the story in the entertainment world to make the disguise believable.
- **Best Character:** Michael's split between dressing as a man and a woman physically and comically expresses the extreme contradiction within his own character.
- **Conflict:** Michael fights Julie, Ron, Les, and Sandy about love and honesty.
- **Basic Action:** Male hero impersonates a woman.
- **Character Change:** W = arrogant, a liar, a womanizer. C = by pretending to be a woman, Michael learns to become a better man, capable of real love.
- **Moral Choice:** Michael sacrifices his lucrative acting job and apologizes to Julie for lying.

### The Godfather
- **Premise:** Youngest son takes revenge and becomes the new Godfather.
- **Designing Principle:** Fairy-tale strategy of the youngest of three sons becoming the new king.
- **W-A-C:** W = unconcerned, afraid, mainstream, legitimate, separated. A = takes revenge. C = tyrannical, absolute ruler of the family.

### Star Wars
- **Premise:** Young man uses fighter skills to save a princess and defeat a galactic empire.
- **Challenges:** Introduce many characters quickly across vast space/time; make futuristic world recognizable; create change in a morally good hero.
- **W-A-C:** W = naive, impetuous, paralyzed, unfocused, lacking confidence. A = uses skills as a fighter. C = self-esteem, place among the chosen few, fighter for good.
