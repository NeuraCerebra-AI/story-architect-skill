# Truby Complete Framework Reference

Detailed reference for all storytelling principles from John Truby's "The Anatomy of Story."

---

## 1. The Dramatic Code

Stories are not imitations of life -- they are models of human growth. A story is a living body where every part connects to and affects every other part. The dramatic code refers to the hidden deep structure common to all great stories:

- The story is an **organic unit** -- not a machine with interchangeable parts
- It tracks a **single human being** making difficult moral choices under pressure
- Through those choices and their consequences, the audience **discovers deeper truths** about what it means to be human
- Organic stories grow from the inside out -- character drives plot, world expresses character, symbols emerge from theme

---

## 2. Premise Technique (10 Steps)

1. **Story idea in one sentence** -- the simplest statement of change
2. **Best character** -- who is forced to deal with the central problem and is changed the most?
3. **Central moral choice** -- what single moral problem does this story explore?
4. **Designing principle** -- the unique internal logic connecting every story element
5. **Theme line** -- one sentence stating the moral vision (debatable, not platitude)
6. **W-A-C** -- wish + action + changed person
7. **Desire line** -- the hero's specific concrete goal
8. **Opponent** -- who wants to stop the hero and why?
9. **Possible self-revelation** -- what might the hero learn?
10. **Test: does everything connect?** -- if any element feels imposed, redesign

### Designing Principle Examples
- **The Godfather**: Use the decline of the American mafia family to show the fall of the American dream through three generations
- **Harry Potter**: A boy discovers he has magical powers and attends a school for wizards, growing in self-mastery as challenges escalate
- **Casablanca**: A cynical bar owner in a war-torn city of transition is forced to choose between personal love and political idealism

## 3. Character Web: Complete Reference

### Four Requirements of a Great Hero
1. A fascinating **problem or flaw** that grabs attention
2. At least one **moral weakness** -- the hero is hurting someone else at the start
3. A **compelling desire** -- specific, urgent, high stakes
4. The **capacity to change** -- must be able to learn from the journey

### Character Change Types (Detailed)
| Type | Arc | Example |
|------|-----|---------|
| Child to adult | Innocence to maturity through hardship | David Copperfield, Luke Skywalker |
| Adult to leader | Self-focused to responsible for others | Michael Corleone (inverted), Rick Blaine |
| Cynic to participant | Withdrawn to re-engaged with life | Rick in Casablanca |
| Leader to tyrant | Power corrupts; good intentions become oppression | Michael Corleone, Macbeth |
| Leader to visionary | Power channeled into transcendent purpose | Moses, Gandhi |

### Archetype Reference
| Archetype | Core Quality | Shadow Side | Story Function |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|----------------|
| King/Queen | Order, fertility, blessing | Tyrant, rigid control | Establishes or upholds the system |
| Warrior | Courage, discipline, skill | Berserker, destroyer | Fights for what matters |
| Magician | Transformation, healing, knowledge | Manipulator, dark sorcerer | Changes reality through special knowledge |
| Trickster | Wit, disruption, freedom | Liar, chaos agent | Breaks rules to reveal truth |
| Artist | Creation, vision, beauty | Dreamer disconnected from reality | Makes the invisible visible |
| Lover | Passion, commitment, connection | Obsessive, addicted | Connects people through emotion |
| Rebel | Independence, revolution | Anarchist, nihilist | Challenges unjust systems |

### Four-Corner Opposition
Place four characters at the corners of a value square. Each represents a different approach to the central moral problem:

```
       Character A (Hero)
           /        \
    Value X          Value Y
         /            \
Character B    ---    Character C
    (Opponent)        (Ally/Subplot)
         \            /
    Value Z          Value W
         \            /
       Character D
    (Fake-ally/2nd opponent)
```

All four compete in some way, with different combinations of values creating different kinds of conflict. This is far more powerful than a single hero-villain line.

## 4. Moral Argument: Five Variants

### Variant 1: Good vs. Bad (Standard)
- Hero starts with moral weakness
- Through the journey, hero gains moral self-revelation
- Opponent represents the values the hero must reject
- Hero makes a moral decision that proves the change
- **Example**: Casablanca, It's a Wonderful Life

### Variant 2: Tragedy
- Hero has a moral weakness/need
- Hero gains self-revelation -- but too late
- The revelation comes after an irreversible action
- The moral argument is made through the hero's destruction
- **Example**: Oedipus Rex, Hamlet, The Great Gatsby

### Variant 3: Pathos
- Hero NEVER gains self-revelation
- The audience gains it instead, seeing what the hero cannot
- Creates deep sadness and empathy
- **Example**: Death of a Salesman, Streetcar Named Desire

### Variant 4: Satire / Irony
- NO character gains self-revelation
- The gap between what characters think and reality IS the point
- Moral argument is made through the audience's recognition of the gap
- Multiple characters represent different wrong approaches
- **Example**: Dr. Strangelove, Network

### Variant 5: Black Comedy
- The moral argument becomes absurd
- Characters act immorally but in a world where morality itself is revealed as a construct
- Often combines with satire
- The audience laughs at moral horror
- **Example**: Pulp Fiction, Fargo, Goodfellas

## 5. Story World: Complete Reference

### Arena Strategies
1. **Large umbrella, then crosscut and condense**: Establish broadest scope early, then focus on subworlds within it (Casablanca, Star Wars)
2. **Single-line journey**: Hero travels through similar terrain that develops along a recognizable line (Titanic, The African Queen)
3. **Circular journey**: Hero departs home and returns, highlighting character change against unchanged world (Wizard of Oz, Finding Nemo)
4. **Fish out of water**: Hero jumps from one arena to a superficially different one where same talents apply (Beverly Hills Cop, Witness)

### Natural Settings and Their Meanings
| Setting | Inherent Meaning | Storytelling Power |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Ocean** | Surface: abstract chessboard of contest. Deep: dreamworld/graveyard | Intensifies sense of contest; hides ancient secrets |
| **Outer Space** | Infinite black void highlighting essential qualities | Unlimited adventure; pairs with myth |
| **Forest** | Natural cathedral; contemplation, lovers' retreat; also place of being lost | Mental loss precedes physical death; ghosts hide here |
| **Jungle** | State of nature; suffocation; everything grabs you | Man reduced to beast; evolution made visible |
| **Desert/Ice** | Dying and death; impersonal brutality | Toughening through isolation |
| **Island** | Miniature earth; separated laboratory | Building/testing new forms of living; Utopia/dystopia |
| **Mountain** | Greatness, seclusion, philosophical insight | Revelation; also hierarchy and tyranny |
| **Plain** | Equality, freedom; also mediocrity and herd mentality | Flatness intensifies contest |
| **River** | Path that IS the road; greatest natural storytelling setting | Upriver = barbarism; downriver = civilization and love |

### Weather Symbolism
- **Lightning/thunder**: Passion and death
- **Rain**: Sadness or coziness
- **Wind**: Destruction
- **Fog**: Mystery
- **Sun**: Happiness but also hidden corruption
- **Snow**: Serenity or quiet death

### Artificial Spaces
| Space | Meaning | Story Use |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| **Warm house** | Big, diverse; enough corners for uniqueness | Utopian community (Meet Me in St. Louis) |
| **Terrifying house** | Cocoon become prison; rotted mind or pressure cooker | Gothic ruin or cramped trap (Psycho, Streetcar) |
| **Bar** | Version of house; community in miniature | Utopian community or dystopian waiting room |
| **Road** | Opposite of house; calls to explore | Hero leaves home, tests abilities, returns transformed |
| **City** | Largest man-made microcosm | Often shrunk to institution metaphor |
| **Passageways** | Decompression between extremely different subworlds | Rules are about to change; move through slowly |

### City-as-Nature Combinations
- **City as mountain**: Powerful at top, poor at base (Batman)
- **City as ocean**: Rooftops as waves, characters float in freedom (Mary Poppins)
- **City as jungle**: Three-dimensional but lethal; enemies lurk everywhere (Blade Runner, Spider-Man)
- **City as forest**: Positive; buildings scaled down, human; Utopian neighborhood (Ghostbusters)

### Seven Hero/World Development Patterns
1. **Slavery to greater slavery to FREEDOM** (Star Wars, Shawshank Redemption)
2. **Slavery to greater slavery or DEATH** (Oedipus, Death of a Salesman)
3. **Hero: slavery to death; World: slavery to freedom** (Hamlet, Seven Samurai)
4. **Slavery to temporary freedom to greater slavery/death** (The Wild Bunch, Butch Cassidy)
5. **Freedom to slavery or death** (King Lear, How Green Was My Valley)
6. **Freedom to slavery to freedom** (Meet Me in St. Louis)
7. **Apparent freedom to greater slavery to freedom** (L.A. Confidential, Jurassic Park)

### Visual Seven Steps
Each key structure step should have its own unique visual subworld:
1. **Weakness/need**: Physical manifestation of the hero's weakness or fear
2. **Desire**: Subworld where hero expresses his goal
3. **Opponent**: Extreme version of the hero's world of slavery
4. **Apparent defeat**: Narrowest space yet; all forces pressing in
5. **Visit to death**: Underworld or place representing decline, aging, death
6. **Battle**: Most confined place of entire story; pressure-cooker effect
7. **Freedom/slavery**: World's endpoint in physical terms

## 6. Symbol Web: Construction Guide

### Building the Symbol Web (Step by Step)
1. **Start with the designing principle** -- derive the symbol line (single connecting thread)
2. **Create the story symbol** -- one image expressing the entire narrative
3. **Examine the character web** before creating any character symbols
4. **Begin with hero vs. main opponent** opposition
5. **Choose symbols representing each character's defining principle** (or its reverse)
6. **Apply symbol opposition within complex characters** for depth
7. **Repeat every symbol** throughout the story with slight variations
8. **Build a web of symbolic objects** related by the designing principle
9. **Connect symbolic actions** to theme (use sparingly -- they call attention to themselves)

### Symbolic Character Categories
- **Gods**: Connect character to divine qualities (creator, controller, judge)
- **Animals**: Ground character in biologically driven behavior (wolf, bird, spider)
- **Machines**: Superhuman strength without feeling -- best when REVERSED (the machine proves most human)

### Symbolic Theme: Advanced Technique
- Create an image expressing a series of actions that hurt others
- Even more powerful: an image expressing TWO moral sequences in conflict
- Place the most powerful thematic symbol last, out of chronological order but in perfect structural order

### Genre Symbol Webs
- **Myth**: Journey, labyrinth, garden, tree, talisman, underground
- **Horror**: Light vs. dark; cross vs. dark animals (wolf, bat, snake)
- **Western**: Horseman, six-gun, hat, badge/star, fence
- **All genres**: Use standard symbols but TWIST their meaning (undercutting the genre)

## 7. 22-Step Plot: Extended Notes

### Opening Types (Detailed)
- **Running start** (most common): Strong ghost, slavery world, serious weaknesses (psychological + moral), multiple problems. Catches the reader immediately.
- **Community start**: Hero lives in paradise of harmony. No ghost. Happy but vulnerable to attack from without or within.
- **Slow start** (extremely difficult): Purposeless hero must discover true desire. First three-quarters have no goal and no narrative drive. Only for master storytellers.

### Revelations: The Engine of Plot
- **Logical order**: Revelations occur in the sequence the hero would most likely discover them
- **Building intensity**: Each revelation must be stronger than the last
- **Increasing pace**: Greater density of surprise near the end heightens drama
- **Reversal**: The most powerful form -- turns understanding of everything on its head (The Sixth Sense, The Usual Suspects)
- **Target**: 7-10 major reveals for a feature; more for detective/thriller stories

### The Iceberg Opponent
1. Create a hierarchy of opponents with alliances (main opponent at pyramid's top)
2. Hide the hierarchy and each opponent's true agenda
3. Reveal this information in pieces at increasing pace
4. Hero faces an obvious opponent early; discovers stronger hidden opposition as conflict intensifies

### Subplot Rules
- Must affect the hero's main plot (if it doesn't, it creates two simultaneous stories)
- Subplot character is usually NOT the ally (different functions)
- Can only cover the 7 key steps
- Introduce as early as naturally appropriate
- Improves character, theme, and texture but slows narrative drive

### Double Reversal
- At self-revelation, BOTH hero AND opponent undergo change
- Each learns from the other
- Give both hero and opponent a weakness and need
- Make the opponent human (capable of learning)
- Connect their two revelations
- Express the author's moral vision as the best of what both learn

## 8. Scene Weave: Extended Techniques

### Storyteller Device (When Applicable)
- The storyteller is probably the true main character
- Introduce the storyteller in a dramatic situation
- The storyteller should NOT be all-knowing at the outset
- The act of telling should lead to a self-revelation
- End the storytelling frame about three-quarters through
- Consider exploring how storytelling can be immoral or destructive

### Crosscutting Techniques
- **Suspense crosscut**: Jump between action lines with accelerating pace
- **Thematic crosscut**: Compare two lines of action, expanding from individual to societal pattern
- **Content crosscut**: Show simultaneous events whose contrast creates meaning (baptism/assassination in The Godfather)
- **Structural crosscut**: Interleave slow and fast plotlines so neither stalls the story

### Multistrand Stories
- 3-5 major storylines, each with its own hero
- Quality comes from juxtaposition of plotlines
- Each plotline covers at least the 7 key steps
- Making the hero of one line the opponent of another prevents sprawl
- Show only the punch scenes of each plotline

## 9. Scene Construction: Extended Reference

### Nine-Step Scene Sequence
1. **Position on character arc** -- where does this scene sit in the hero's development?
2. **Problems** -- what must this scene accomplish for the story?
3. **Strategy** -- the author's meta-plan for solving those problems
4. **Desire** -- whose desire drives this scene? (not necessarily the hero)
5. **Endpoint** -- how does the driving desire resolve? Know this in advance.
6. **Opponent** -- who opposes and what do they specifically fight about?
7. **Plan** -- direct (states openly what he wants; increases conflict) or indirect (pretends to want something else; may cause greater conflict later)
8. **Conflict** -- must build to breaking point or solution; escalate, not meander
9. **Twist or reveal** -- surprise for characters, audience, or both

### Symphonic Dialogue: Three-Track Method
Write each scene three times:
1. **First pass**: Story dialogue only (the sketch)
2. **Second pass**: Add moral dialogue (values in conflict beneath the story talk)
3. **Third pass**: Thread in key words, phrases, taglines (cumulative thematic resonance)

### Track 1: Story Dialogue Details
- Character 1 states desire; Character 2 opposes
- They escalate through plans and counter-plans
- Advanced: shift from dialogue about ACTION to dialogue about BEING ("You are...")
- This deepens the scene -- characters frame actions as definitions of identity

### Track 2: Moral Dialogue Details
- One character proposes action; other opposes on moral grounds
- Each attacks and defends with reasons
- At highest level: compare two entire WAYS OF LIFE
- Values should ride on the back of story dialogue to avoid preachiness

### Track 3: Key Words / Taglines Details
- Words carrying special symbolic/thematic meaning
- Deployed through repetition and variation across the story
- Use the word far more often than normal conversation warrants
- Cumulative meaning accrues in the audience's mind
- Examples: "I'll make him an offer he can't refuse"; "May the Force be with you"

### Complex / Subtext Scenes
- Subtext is NOT always superior -- if maximum conflict is needed, directness may be stronger
- For subtext: give multiple characters hidden desires in direct conflict
- All characters with hidden desires use indirect plans (subterfuge)
- Interplay of multiple hidden desires and indirect plans = dense unspoken tension

## 10. The Never-Ending Story

### Infinite Story Tapestry Elements
- **Unfulfilled hero desire with new desires emerging**: Prevents system from closing
- **Surprising change in opponent or minor character**: Audience re-experiences story with that character as true hero
- **Dense background detail**: On first read sits in background; on rereading moves to foreground
- **Textural richness**: Elements more interesting once plot is known
- **Shifting storyteller-character relationship**: Reads differently once plot is known
- **Ambiguous moral argument / withheld final moral choice**: Forces audience to explore in their own lives

### False Endings to Avoid
- **Premature ending**: Early self-revelation kills development; desire achieved too quickly starts new story
- **Arbitrary ending**: Story just stops -- nothing was developing, so no sense of fruition
- **Closed ending**: Goal accomplished, simple revelation, calm equilibrium -- shuts down the living quality

### The Ultimate Principle
A great story is a Mobius strip. Since the organic end is found in the beginning, it signals the audience to go back to the start and experience it again -- each time different, because the audience is always rethinking in light of what just happened. The story literally keeps telling itself.

## Time Techniques in Story World

### Seasons
- **Summer**: Freedom/vulnerability
- **Fall**: Decline
- **Winter**: Lowest point
- **Spring**: Rise
- Covering all four creates circular story emphasizing how things remain the same
- Cutting against the expected sequence asserts that humans are not enslaved by natural patterns

### Holidays and Rituals
- A ritual is a philosophy translated into repeated actions -- already dramatic
- The writer must decide whether to support or attack the ritual's philosophy
- Use holidays/rituals as structural markers

### The Single Day
- 12-hour clock: funnel of urgency
- 24-hour clock: circularity; most things remain the same, making changes significant
- "Perfect day" variation: communal activity connected to natural time = Utopian harmony

### Ticking Clock
- Audience told action must complete by specific time
- Provides intense narrative drive at expense of texture
- Creates fastest funnel; allows hundreds of characters to act with urgency
- Most common in action, thriller, caper stories

## Five Movement Shapes

Every story follows one of these deep patterns:
1. **Linear**: A leads to B leads to C (cause and effect chain)
2. **Meandering**: Wandering path with detours (picaresque, odyssey)
3. **Spiral**: Returns to similar situations at deeper levels each time
4. **Branching**: Multiple paths explored (ensemble, multiplot)
5. **Explosive**: Starts at center and radiates outward (mystery, revelation)

Choose the movement shape that best serves the designing principle.
